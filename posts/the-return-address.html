Story

The Return Address

A story, a little imagined · December 2020 – July 2022

I. The mouse who did not come home

December 2020When the mouse disappeared, nobody told the girl how long he would be gone.

At first, they said a few days. Then they said he had been moved to another room, which seemed reasonable. People were moved from one room to another all the time. She had lived in so many rooms herself that she had stopped remembering which window belonged to which house.

But the mouse had never liked moving.

He preferred his place on the sofa in Sadbury, where the cushions were large and he was small, and where he could disappear without actually going anywhere.

The princess used to complain that he spent too much time there.

Now that he was gone, the sofa looked enormous.

The girl wondered whether someone should measure it.

She had learned that when things changed unexpectedly, measurements were useful. They could prove that something had happened, even when nobody agreed on what it was.

She measured nothing.

★ ★ ★

The mouse had been taken to a house where people were supposed to recover.

It was a very large house, with long corridors, white walls, and a great many people who knew things but could not explain them.

The girl called.

A man told her the mouse was doing well and would soon be coming home.

She was relieved.

The following day, a woman told her he was not doing well at all.

The girl was surprised.

– But yesterday he was coming home.

– Things change, said the woman.

– In one day?

– Sometimes much faster.

This was true. The girl could not disagree with it.

She asked what she could do.

– Pray, said the woman.

The girl had been asked to pray many times before, usually by the princess, who believed that almost everything could be improved by asking someone invisible to intervene.

She had never found the method particularly reliable.

Still, she considered it.

– Is there nothing else?

The woman did not know.

The girl thanked her and hung up.

She had become very polite.

★ ★ ★

She called the mouse several times.

He did not answer.

Eventually, someone answered for him.

The girl asked to speak to her father. There was a long silence, and then a small noise, and then a voice.

It was the mouse.

Or rather, it was what remained of his voice after somebody had taken most of the air out of it.

– How are you, Dad?

– I’m all right. Don’t worry.

He explained that he was in a room with other people. Everything was fine.

The girl had heard this before. The mouse had always been fine.

When the princess shouted, he was fine.

When the girl was upset, he was fine.

When he fell asleep with a cigarette and nearly burned the house down, he was fine, although the sofa had disagreed.

Now he was fine again.

The girl cried.

She told him to take care of himself.

He said something else, but there was hardly any breath left in the words.

She would spend a great deal of time afterwards trying to remember them.

★ ★ ★

Then, one day, the people in the large house announced that the mouse would not be coming home.

They had tried, they said.

They had done what they could.

The girl wanted to know exactly what this meant.

What had they tried? How many times? Who had been there? Had he been frightened? Had he asked for anything?

The answers were either unavailable or not useful.

Eventually, someone explained that she could collect his identity card.

This was an interesting development.

Apparently, the mouse was no longer permitted to exist, but his identity was still available for collection.

The girl went to collect it.

A woman gave her the card and explained some things.

The girl listened.

She wanted to ask whether the mouse had been alone.

Instead, she looked at the little photograph.

There he was.

The mouse had always looked slightly uncomfortable in photographs. This one was no exception.

She put the card away.

There were no further instructions.

II. A very small inheritance

For a while, the girl did not write.

She had written about nearly everything since she was small: unpleasant examinations, strange conversations, dreams, books she had not finished, people she had liked too much, and people she had liked considerably less.

She had even written about the time she had washed her hands ten times after eating flatbread.

But she could not write about the mouse.

She could not explain what had happened, even to herself.

At night she dreamed about him.

In the dreams he was usually doing something ordinary. Sitting on the sofa. Smoking. Walking through a room. Sometimes he was younger. Sometimes he looked exactly as he had looked the last time she saw him.

Then she woke up.

For several seconds she forgot.

These were good seconds.

They were followed by the others.

★ ★ ★

Mr. Rabbit sat on her desk.

He had been living with her for years, having been purchased with money intended for more important things, and he had developed strong opinions on almost everything.

– You ought to write something, he said.

– What?

– Anything.

– That is not very helpful.

– I am a rabbit, he replied. There are limits.

The Mistale sat beside him.

It had grown quite large over the years, and its pockets were full of sentences the girl had crossed out.

Lately, it had been following her even more closely than usual.

– Perhaps you could write what happened, it suggested.

– I don’t know what happened.

– Then write that.

She considered this.

It was the first sensible suggestion either of them had made in some time.

★ ★ ★

She opened a notebook.

Hello, Dad.

Then she stopped.

Someone came into the room and asked what she was writing.

– Some ideas, she said.

This was not entirely untrue.

She had several ideas.

Most of them concerned things that could no longer be done.

★ ★ ★

As she continued writing, she noticed something peculiar.

Her handwriting looked like the mouse’s.

The same slant. The same hurried letters. The same way certain words seemed to lean on the words beside them.

She had never noticed it before.

She looked at old papers he had written and compared them carefully with her own.

It was unmistakable.

The princess had given her a great many things, including her opinion of almost everything.

But the handwriting had come from the mouse.

The girl was pleased.

She wondered whether handwriting counted as an inheritance.

There was probably an office where such things could be established.

She did not intend to visit it.

III. The princess and the missing mouse

The princess was very upset.

She had loved the mouse, although she had spent much of their life together explaining what was wrong with him.

Now she spoke about him often.

Sometimes she cried.

Sometimes she became angry.

Sometimes she explained that the girl should stop making such a fuss about things, because everybody had problems and it was important to be strong.

The girl tried.

She had been trying to be strong since she was little, when the princess’s shadow covered the wall and the mouse sat quietly at her feet.

Back then she had thought that being strong meant not crying.

Later she thought it meant not complaining.

Now she was beginning to suspect that being strong meant not requiring anything from anybody.

This was an unfortunate definition, because she required several things.

She did not mention them.

★ ★ ★

The mouse had been small, but his absence was remarkably large.

When he was alive, the princess called him for everything.

He listened.

Sometimes he agreed.

Sometimes he rolled his eyes when the princess was not looking.

Sometimes he told the girl what the princess wanted, and sometimes he told the princess what the girl had said.

He had not always been very helpful.

The girl knew this.

She had been angry with him many times for sitting still when he should have stood up.

But he had also remembered things.

The princess’s birthday, for example.

He would remind the girl in advance.

He was particularly fond of birthdays, especially his own.

The girl had never understood why. She found the annual celebration of becoming older a little strange, particularly when people spent the rest of the year complaining about their age.

But the mouse liked it.

He liked being remembered.

Now there was nobody to remind her.

★ ★ ★

Among the mouse’s things, the girl discovered that he had called the princess principessa.

She looked at the word for a long time.

It was affectionate.

She had not expected that.

The princess was a wolf, of course. The girl had known this for years.

But the mouse had loved her.

Apparently, a person could know perfectly well what a wolf was and love it anyway.

The girl did not know what to do with this information.

The Mistale offered to keep it.

– No, she said.

– Why not?

– Because I haven’t understood it yet.

The Mistale looked disappointed.

It liked unfinished things.

There were so many of them.

IV. Six months

July 2021Six months passed.

The girl found this very difficult to believe.

She had been living far away from Sadbury for years. Sometimes she went home, sometimes she did not. Six months without seeing the mouse had once been perfectly ordinary.

Now the same six months had become something else.

She tried to understand why.

The number had not changed.

Six was still six.

The months were still months.

But before, six months had meant that she might see him again.

Now it meant that she would not.

She wrote to him.

Hello, Dad.

I wish it had only been six months since I last saw you. Not six months since I learned that I would never see you again.

She read the sentence several times.

It was correct.

She wished it were not.

★ ★ ★

The girl had heard that grief came in stages.

She had looked them up.

There were five, according to some people, and seven according to others. She imagined a staircase with an uncertain number of steps.

Apparently, one was supposed to climb it.

She wondered whether skipping a step was permitted.

Mr. Rabbit thought it would depend on the staircase.

The Mistale wanted to know whether the steps were numbered.

The girl closed the book.

She had never been particularly good at following instructions that could not agree among themselves.

★ ★ ★

She dreamed about the mouse again.

He was sitting on the old sofa, half hidden by the cushions, smoking.

She knew she was dreaming.

She went over to him.

– Dad?

He looked up.

– Yes?

She wanted to ask him why he had gone.

But she had asked herself that question so many times that it seemed unreasonable to ask him too.

Instead, she sat down.

They stayed there for a while.

When she woke up, she could almost remember what the room smelled like.

She was pleased.

Then she remembered why.

V. The things she had not done

3 December 2021The girl had begun keeping a list.

It was not an ordinary list, with things to buy or things to finish.

It was a list of things she should have done.

She should have called sooner.

She should have called more often.

She should have asked different questions.

She should have found another doctor, another telephone number, another way of getting someone to listen.

She should have gone to Sadbury.

She should have insisted.

She should have known.

The list became very long.

Mr. Rabbit examined it.

– Some of these things are the same, he said.

– I know.

– You have written them several times.

– I know.

– Shall I cross out the repetitions?

– No.

The Mistale, who had been listening, looked relieved.

★ ★ ★

She thought about the last telephone call.

The mouse had told her not to worry.

She had cried.

She had told him to take care.

Then she had hung up.

Why had she hung up?

Why had she not said something else?

She tried to remember whether she had told him she loved him.

She could not remember.

It was a terrible thing, she thought, to forget the words of a conversation that could never be repeated.

She had remembered much less important conversations perfectly.

Once, a woman on a train had told her an entire story about a boy, and she still remembered the woman’s buttons, her hat, the smell of her clothes.

But now, when it mattered, her memory had become unreliable.

She was furious with it.

Memory was not impressed.

★ ★ ★

That evening she wrote another letter.

Dad, I dream about you.

It feels as if I see you all the time.

As if I am going to see you soon.

I know that this is not possible.

But somehow I still think it is.

She stopped.

There was something wrong with the last two sentences.

They contradicted each other.

She considered correcting them.

Then she remembered the Mistale.

She left them as they were.

VI. The birthday

July 2022The mouse would have had another birthday.

The girl had been waiting for it.

She did not know why.

He was not going to attend.

Still, the date arrived, as dates tend to do, without consulting the people concerned.

In Sadbury, the princess was talking about something that had happened to her.

The girl listened.

She wanted to talk about the mouse.

But whenever she tried, the conversation became about the princess, or about strength, or about the correct way to live after something terrible had happened.

Eventually, she stopped trying.

She had learned that the princess was very good at explaining how other people should behave.

She had learned this many years before.

★ ★ ★

The girl opened her notebook.

Hello, Dad.

I’m sorry.

I feel as though I didn’t do anything.

She looked at the sentence.

Then she wrote it again.

I didn’t do anything.

It was not entirely true.

She had made calls.

She had looked for information.

She had asked questions and listened to answers that changed from one day to another.

But none of these things had brought the mouse home.

She could not decide whether an action that failed to produce the desired result should still count as an action.

At the School of Numbers, she would once have known how to answer.

She was not at the School of Numbers anymore.

★ ★ ★

She thought of the princess.

For years, she had blamed her for so many things.

Some of them deserved blame.

Some of them probably did not.

The girl was quite capable of distinguishing between the two, although doing so made her life more complicated.

Now she wondered whether she had blamed the princess for things that belonged to somebody else.

And whether the mouse had been caught between them.

He had loved the princess.

The girl knew this.

She did not understand it completely, but she knew it.

She wrote:

Dad, I’m sorry that I blamed her so often.

I know you loved her.

Then she put down the pen.

She did not want to forgive everything.

She only wanted him back.

These were different things.

VII. The post office

The girl kept writing letters.

She wrote them in Romanian, although she had lived away from Sadbury for many years and had become accustomed to thinking in other languages.

Romanian was the language in which she had first learned to be frightened.

It was also the language in which the mouse had spoken to her.

She did not know whether that mattered.

She suspected it did.

★ ★ ★

Years later, when the road took her through the Country of Mistales, she found a post office at the top of a hill.

The postmaster was an enormous owl wearing a hat that was much too small.

He looked at the bundle of letters in her hands.

– Destination?

The girl hesitated.

– I’m not sure.

– Name of recipient?

She told him.

The owl examined a register.

– I see. And the address?

– I don’t have one.

– Then how do you expect us to deliver them?

– I don’t know.

The owl sighed.

He had worked in correspondence for a very long time and had met a great many people who did not know where their letters were going.

Most of them were in love.

The others were considerably more difficult.

– Is the recipient expecting them?

– I don’t think so.

– Has he moved?

– In a manner of speaking.

– Is there a forwarding address?

– That’s what I wanted to ask you.

The owl took off his little hat.

– My dear girl, he said, we are a postal service. We are not responsible for finding people who no longer have an address.

– Then who is?

– That is a very good question.

He put his hat back on.

Apparently, the discussion was over.

★ ★ ★

The girl turned to leave.

Then she stopped.

– What happens to letters that cannot be delivered?

The owl pointed to a door behind the counter.

Inside was a room filled with letters.

Some were tied in bundles. Others had slipped from their envelopes. A few were moving slowly across the floor, looking for places to sit.

There were letters to old lovers, dead grandparents, lost friends, children who had grown up, and people who had disappeared without explaining themselves.

Some had been written years after their recipients were gone.

Others had never been finished.

In one corner, a small letter was crying.

The Mistale went over to comfort it.

Mr. Rabbit looked around.

– Very untidy, he said.

– We do our best, said the owl.

– It doesn’t look like it.

The owl chose not to respond.

The girl held her letters tightly.

– Can I leave these here?

– Of course.

– Will anybody read them?

– I couldn’t say.

– Will they reach him?

The owl looked at her.

For once, he did not have a form to consult.

– I don’t know, he said.

She appreciated the answer.

It was the first one she had received that did not pretend to be something else.

She decided to keep the letters.

VIII. The same handwriting

On her way home, the girl passed through the forest where the well stood.

The well had been there since she was little.

It had refused her water once, when she was on her way out of Sadbury, and offered it years later, when she returned.

She stopped beside it.

The water was clear.

She looked down.

For a moment she thought she could see the mouse sitting at the bottom, very small, with his cigarette and his old clothes.

She leaned closer.

It was only her reflection.

She was disappointed, although she could hardly blame the well.

★ ★ ★

At home, the princess had sent another message.

It was a religious picture with golden letters and a promise that everything happened for a reason.

The girl looked at it.

She had never liked this explanation.

A reason was something that could be examined. It was something you could disagree with, test, or sometimes prove wrong.

But the reasons people spoke of after somebody died were different.

They were invisible, and therefore very difficult to argue with.

She put the telephone away.

Mr. Rabbit had fallen asleep.

The Mistale was sitting beside the notebook, turning its empty pockets inside out.

– Have you finished? it asked.

– No.

– What are you going to write?

The girl thought about it.

She wanted to write that the mouse had been kind.

But he had not always been kind.

She wanted to write that he had protected her.

But he had not always protected her.

She wanted to write that she had done everything she could.

But she did not know whether she had.

She wanted to write that she was all right.

She was not sure about that either.

In the end, she wrote nothing of the sort.

She opened a new page.

Hello, Dad.

The letters leaned slightly to the right.

The same as his.

She looked at the page for a long time.

Then she began telling him about her day.
