Story

The Fifth Bed

A story, a little imagined · a city between two hills

Long before the girl encountered the Mistales, and even longer before she arrived in the Kingdom of Borrowed Wings, she moved to a city built between two hills.

On the first hill stood an enormous school where everything had to be counted. The inhabitants spent their days measuring distances, arranging numbers, and proving that things existed.

On the second hill stood another school, where people drew things that did not exist and sometimes insisted that the drawings were more important than the things themselves.

The two schools had been arguing for centuries.

The first maintained that art was useless because nobody could agree on its meaning. The second maintained that numbers were ugly because they always ended up looking the same.

The girl liked both.

She applied to both schools and, rather unexpectedly, was admitted to both.

At the School of Numbers, her name appeared near the bottom of an enormous list nailed to a wall. She had travelled a long way to see it. For a moment, she was certain it wasn’t there, and she searched again, beginning at the top.

Then she found it.

She was delighted.

The people whose names appeared higher up seemed equally delighted, although she couldn’t understand why being admitted first was necessarily better than being admitted last. They would all be attending the same school.

At the School of Pictures, nobody remembered exactly how she had been admitted. There had been an examination involving a complicated camera and several wooden objects that she had been instructed to photograph in motion.

She had taken a picture of something.

She couldn’t remember what.

Apparently, it had been satisfactory.

The schools had one rule in common: a person was expected to choose.

The girl had never been particularly good at choosing between things she liked.

So she enrolled in both.

Her family agreed to help pay for one of them, and the girl promised to manage the rest.

She did not yet know how much the rest would cost.

Neither did anyone else.

This was perhaps why the arrangement was permitted.

· · ·

Finding a place to sleep proved more complicated than entering two schools.

The city had many houses, but most were occupied by people who had arrived earlier, knew somebody important, or had been born in them.

The schools operated several boarding houses for students, although the beds were distributed according to rules that changed depending on who was asking.

The girl was entitled to a bed under one rule and not entitled to it under another.

After several days of searching, she met a girl who wished to sell her place in one of the boarding houses.

– Can you sell a place? the girl from Sadbury asked.

– Of course.

– But isn’t it yours only because they gave it to you?

– Exactly.

The girl thought about this.

It seemed that a thing could belong to somebody without actually belonging to them, and could then be sold to somebody else who would not belong there at all.

This was an interesting economic system.

She paid the requested amount.

The other girl gave her a key and explained that, should anyone ask, the bed was still hers.

– Even when I’m sleeping in it?

– Especially then.

The girl from Sadbury accepted the arrangement.

She had bought many things in her life, but never before the right to pretend to be somebody else while unconscious.

· · ·

The boarding house was a large, tired building with narrow corridors and windows that had been painted shut.

At the entrance sat an elderly woman who guarded an enormous book.

The book contained the names of everybody officially permitted to sleep in the building.

– Name? asked the woman.

The girl gave it.

The woman searched the book.

– You’re not here.

– I know.

– Then why are you here?

– I have a bed.

– That’s not the same thing.

The girl showed her the key.

The woman examined it.

– The key is genuine.

– Thank you.

– But you aren’t.

The girl considered explaining the arrangement, then decided that some explanations only created additional problems.

She went upstairs.

· · ·

Her room contained five beds.

Four were arranged in two pairs, one above the other. The fifth stood alone, close to the floor, looking slightly embarrassed by its lack of ambition.

The girl immediately chose it.

She had once climbed onto a high bed and discovered that her stomach strongly objected to the distance between herself and the ground.

She considered this an unreasonable objection, since the ground remained exactly where it had always been.

Nevertheless, she respected it.

The fifth bed was perfect.

It didn’t require climbing.

It had no ladder.

And if she fell out during the night, there wasn’t very far to go.

She placed her bag beside it and sat down.

For the first time since arriving in the city, she had somewhere to be.

The other four inhabitants arrived gradually.

The first girl carried a small bell around her neck and spoke as though she had been appointed responsible for everybody’s moral development.

She believed strongly in rules, especially those that had not been written down.

The second was small and gentle. She had an extraordinary talent for agreeing with people before discovering what they wanted.

The third was tall and appeared to have a cloud permanently attached to her head. Sometimes it rained a little when she was unhappy, although she apologised whenever this happened.

The fourth spoke quietly, usually after everybody else had left the room. She seemed to have formed opinions about most things but considered sharing them a waste of effort.

The girl from Sadbury regarded them with suspicion.

This was unfair, since none had done anything particularly suspicious.

But she had grown up with the princess, and the princess had taught her that a woman could say something perfectly ordinary while meaning something entirely different.

The girl had never managed to determine which women followed this principle.

She therefore assumed that all of them might.

It was an inconvenient method of making friends.

The first girl introduced herself and asked whether the newcomer believed in the Great Invisible Ear.

– Which ear?

– The one that hears everything.

The girl looked toward the ceiling.

– Does it have another one?

– What?

– Ears usually come in pairs.

The first girl did not find this amusing.

The girl from Sadbury decided that perhaps she should speak less.

This was a decision she had made many times before.

It had never produced particularly satisfactory results, but she continued trying.

The four girls quickly became friends.

They ate together, borrowed each other’s clothes, exchanged secrets, and developed a complicated system for deciding who had offended whom.

The girl from Sadbury observed this with interest.

She had not known that friendship required so much administration.

Sometimes the girls invited her to join them.

Sometimes they didn’t.

Usually, she declined before discovering whether she had been invited.

She preferred her headphones.

They were connected to a little music player by a long wire that frequently became tangled around her arms, the bed, and occasionally other people.

The wire was useful.

It gave her a reason not to hear things.

Unfortunately, she couldn’t wear the headphones in the bathroom. The wire was not long enough, and she was afraid of dropping the player into the sink.

Those were the most socially dangerous minutes of her day.

One morning, the first girl asked her why she was always so quiet.

– I don’t have anything to say.

– Everybody has something to say.

– Then perhaps I’ve already said mine.

The first girl looked disappointed.

The girl from Sadbury put her headphones back on.

She wondered why people insisted that she speak and then appeared dissatisfied with what came out.

· · ·

The boarding house had a communal kitchen at the end of the corridor.

It was inhabited by several pots, an elderly kettle, and a small Nibbling that lived beneath the stove.

The Nibbling had six legs and a mouth in the middle of its stomach. It ate whatever fell on the floor and occasionally pieces of the floor itself.

Nobody knew who had brought it there.

The girl found it one evening chewing the corner of a cupboard.

– You’re not supposed to eat that.

– Why?

– Because it belongs to the house.

– So?

– You can’t eat other people’s things.

The Nibbling considered this.

– Who owns the dust?

– What?

– The dust. Who owns it?

– Nobody, I suppose.

– Then I shall eat the dust.

It continued chewing the cupboard.

The girl decided not to pursue the matter.

She had come to prepare dinner.

Her cooking arrangements were simple. She possessed a bag of pasta and another bag containing shredded cheese.

She boiled one and poured the other over it.

Occasionally she reversed the order, but the results were disappointing.

She drank enormous quantities of a dark, sweet liquid that came in red bottles, and made coffee from little sachets containing a powder that resembled dust but was considerably more expensive.

She preferred the kitchen when nobody else was there.

Unfortunately, the other inhabitants of the boarding house also needed to eat.

She began visiting at increasingly strange hours.

Sometimes she waited until midnight.

Sometimes she ate early.

Once, she encountered the Nibbling again.

It had eaten the handle of the kitchen door.

– Now how am I supposed to close it?

– You could leave it open.

– But then people will come in.

– They come in anyway.

The girl looked at it.

She was beginning to find the creature irritating.

She also suspected that it was right.

· · ·

Life between the two hills was exhausting.

Every morning, the girl climbed to the School of Pictures, carrying things that had to be drawn.

Then she ran downhill to the School of Numbers, carrying things that had to be calculated.

The teachers on the first hill insisted that she should feel more.

The teachers on the second insisted that she should think more.

She tried to do both.

This proved difficult because feeling often interrupted thinking, and thinking frequently made the feelings worse.

At the School of Pictures, students sat in groups discussing beauty, meaning, and the importance of expressing oneself.

At the School of Numbers, they discussed problems that had answers.

The girl preferred the problems with answers.

However, she liked the people on the hill without answers.

This was inconvenient.

She found herself running from one school to the other, never quite certain where she belonged.

Sometimes, halfway down the hill, she stopped and looked at both buildings.

They seemed perfectly content to remain where they were.

She envied them.

One evening, the four girls announced that they would spend the night outside a chapel.

An important relic had arrived in the city, and people were forming a queue to touch it.

The first girl was very excited.

She explained that the relic had extraordinary powers.

– What does it do? asked the girl from Sadbury.

– Many things.

– Which things?

– It depends.

The girl was becoming familiar with answers of this kind.

The second girl agreed to accompany her friend.

– Is it good to go? she asked.

– Of course.

– Then I’ll go.

The girl from Sadbury wondered whether the second girl would have agreed to sleep outside a bakery if somebody had told her it was important.

Probably.

She declined the invitation.

The first girl asked why.

– Because it’s cold.

– That’s not a good reason.

The girl thought it was an excellent reason.

But she had learned that reasons were not necessarily judged by their usefulness.

The following morning, the two girls returned exhausted and very pleased.

They had touched the relic.

– Did anything happen? asked the girl from Sadbury.

– Yes.

– What?

– We touched it.

The girl nodded.

She was glad the journey had been successful.

· · ·

Winter arrived.

The room became cold, and the girls began collecting blankets, coats, scarves and small arguments.

The girl from Sadbury kept most of her belongings in her bag.

She did not occupy much space in the cupboard because she disliked the idea of somebody moving her things.

She did not place photographs on the walls.

She did not leave books on the table.

She rarely invited anyone into the room, and when people came to visit the other girls, she put on her headphones and became as unobtrusive as possible.

Eventually, something peculiar began happening to the fifth bed.

At first, it moved slightly closer to the wall.

The girl assumed somebody had pushed it.

Then its legs grew shorter.

The mattress became narrower.

One morning, she woke with one arm hanging over the edge, although she was certain she had fallen asleep in the middle.

She measured it.

The bed had become smaller.

She asked the girl with the cloud whether she had noticed.

– Perhaps you’re getting bigger, said the cloud girl.

This was possible.

The girl had been eating rather badly.

She decided to drink less of the sweet liquid.

It did not help.

· · ·

Every Thursday, a creature called the Registrar visited the boarding house.

The Registrar had the body of a large moth, the face of an elderly accountant, and a collection of spectacles hanging from its wings.

Its duty was to ensure that the number of inhabitants corresponded exactly to the number recorded in the Book.

It was very proud of this responsibility.

– Four, it announced at the entrance to their room.

The girls looked at one another.

There were five of them.

– Four, repeated the Registrar.

It made a mark in the Book and departed.

The girl from Sadbury was relieved.

She had no wish to explain why she was there.

The following Thursday, the Registrar returned.

– Four.

Again, it left.

The third Thursday, the Registrar looked directly at the girl from Sadbury.

She held her breath.

– Four, it said.

Then it closed the Book.

The girl began to suspect that the Registrar could not see her.

This was both convenient and rather disturbing.

· · ·

One morning, the fifth bed disappeared.

The girl returned from school and found an empty space beside the wall.

Her bag remained on the floor.

Her pillow lay beneath the window.

Everything else was gone.

She asked the others what had happened.

– The Registrar came, said the first girl.

– It said there was an extra bed.

– But there are five of us.

– Apparently there are four.

The girl stared at the empty space.

For a moment, she considered sleeping on the floor.

It would not be particularly uncomfortable. She had slept on floors before.

But something about the disappearance troubled her.

She had paid for the bed.

She had slept in it for months.

She knew its little noises, the unevenness of its mattress, and the way the frame creaked when she turned over.

It had been hers in every way that mattered to a sleeping person.

She decided to complain.

The Office of Beds was located in the basement of the boarding house, between a locked laundry room and a staircase that led nowhere.

Behind a desk sat a small man with an enormous nose.

He wore a badge reading Assistant Registrar of Resting Places.

The girl explained her problem.

– My bed has disappeared.

– Beds do not disappear.

– Mine has.

– Then it probably wasn’t yours.

– It was in my room.

– That doesn’t establish ownership.

– I slept in it.

– People sleep in all sorts of places.

The girl considered this.

He was right.

She had once fallen asleep on a train and woken up in another country.

– But I paid for it.

The man looked interested.

– Do you have a receipt?

– No.

– Then how do you know you paid?

– Because I remember.

– Memory is not an accepted form of payment.

The girl sighed.

She could see that the discussion was going to be difficult.

The man opened an enormous register.

– Your name?

She told him.

He searched.

– You’re not here.

– I know.

– Then you don’t have a bed.

– But I did.

– That’s impossible.

– Why?

– Because you don’t have one.

The girl leaned back in her chair.

She had encountered this kind of argument before, although usually in conversations with the princess.

– What should I do?

– You must prove that you live here.

– How?

– Bring somebody who can confirm your presence.

– Will that be enough?

– Certainly not. But it is a beginning.

· · ·

She returned upstairs.

The four girls were sitting around the table, eating biscuits and discussing somebody who was not present.

The girl from Sadbury explained the situation.

The first girl said that it was very unfortunate.

The second asked whether she ought to be worried.

The third offered her a blanket.

The fourth said nothing.

– Could one of you come downstairs and explain that I live here?

They looked at one another.

– Of course you live here, said the cloud girl.

– Then will you tell the Registrar?

– But you’re not registered, said the first girl.

– I know.

– And you bought somebody else’s place.

– Yes.

– Then perhaps you shouldn’t have.

The girl felt a familiar irritation rising in her chest.

She did not deny that the arrangement was irregular.

She had known it was irregular from the beginning.

What she could not understand was why the irregularity had suddenly become more important than the fact that she needed somewhere to sleep.

The fourth girl finally spoke.

– I’ll come.

The others looked surprised.

The girl from Sadbury was surprised too.

She had barely spoken to her.

The following morning, they went to the Office of Beds.

The man with the enormous nose listened impatiently.

– Do you know this person? he asked the fourth girl.

– Yes.

– Does she live in your room?

– Yes.

– For how long?

The fourth girl thought.

– I don’t know exactly.

– Then how can you be sure?

– Because she’s there every morning.

The man frowned.

– That is not proof.

– She sleeps beside the wall.

– That proves nothing.

– She leaves her bag on the floor.

– Anybody could leave a bag.

– She makes terrible coffee.

The girl from Sadbury looked offended.

Her coffee was not terrible.

It was merely inexpensive.

The fourth girl continued.

– She never uses the cupboard. She wears headphones. She comes home late and sometimes eats pasta in the middle of the night. She thinks nobody notices, but the kitchen smells of melted cheese.

The girl stared at her.

She had not realised that anybody knew these things.

– She lives there, the fourth girl concluded.

The Registrar examined the Book.

– Nevertheless, the records indicate four.

– Then the records are wrong.

The man looked horrified.

Records, he explained, could not be wrong.

If records were wrong, it would become impossible to know which things were correct.

And if nobody knew which things were correct, the entire boarding house might have to close.

The girl from Sadbury thought this was a considerable amount of responsibility to place on a single bed.

They returned to their room.

The girl sat on the floor.

The fourth girl went back to reading.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then the fourth girl said, You could use one of the bunks.

– They’re all occupied.

– Not all the time.

– But they belong to somebody.

The fourth girl looked at her.

– So did your bed.

The girl considered this.

It was an unpleasantly good observation.

She began unpacking her bag.

She placed her books beside the wall, her clothes on the empty floor, and her music player on the windowsill.

Then, for the first time since arriving at the boarding house, she opened the cupboard and placed several things inside.

The first girl objected.

– That’s my shelf.

– Sorry.

The girl moved her things.

– That’s mine, said the second girl.

She moved them again.

Eventually, she discovered a narrow space at the bottom that nobody seemed to want.

She placed her clothes there.

It wasn’t much.

But it was a beginning.

That evening, the Registrar returned.

It stood in the doorway and examined the room.

– Four, it announced.

The girl from Sadbury stood up.

– Five.

The creature adjusted its spectacles.

– Four.

– Five.

The Registrar opened the Book.

– Four.

The girl pointed at each inhabitant in turn.

– One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

The Registrar counted them.

Then it counted again.

Its wings began to tremble.

– This is very irregular.

– I know.

– There are only four registered occupants.

– Yes.

– But there are five people.

– Apparently.

The Registrar looked increasingly distressed.

Then it noticed the empty space beside the wall.

– Where is the fifth bed?

– You took it.

– Because there were only four inhabitants.

– But now there are five.

The creature stared at her.

For several moments, it seemed unable to decide which problem was more serious: an extra bed or an extra person.

Finally, it closed the Book.

– This will require further investigation.

– Of course.

The Registrar departed.

The girl sat down.

She was beginning to understand how institutions survived.

They postponed things.

The following day, two workers arrived carrying a bed.

It was not the old one.

This bed was narrow, rather ugly, and had been painted an unfortunate shade of brown.

They placed it beside the wall.

– Is this mine? asked the girl.

– No, said one of the workers.

– Then whose is it?

– Nobody’s.

– Why is it here?

– Storage.

The girl looked at the bed.

– May I sleep in it?

The worker shrugged.

– We’re only responsible for delivering it.

Then they left.

That night, the girl slept in the storage bed.

It creaked terribly.

The mattress had a large hollow in the middle.

And one of the legs was slightly shorter than the others.

She liked it.

· · ·

The winter passed.

The girl continued climbing between the two hills. She learned things, failed at others, and began to suspect that the distance between the schools was growing longer.

The four girls continued living their lives around her. They talked, argued, shared things, and sometimes cried. The girl from Sadbury was there too, although she was never entirely sure whether being in the same room counted as being together.

She still wore her headphones.

She still avoided the kitchen when it was crowded.

She still found the first girl rather frightening.

The Nibbling continued eating pieces of the boarding house.

One day, it ate the sign above the entrance that listed the maximum number of inhabitants.

Nobody replaced it.

The girl found this amusing.

And then, eventually, she left.

She never heard from any of them again.

This was a peculiar thing about people. You could sleep beside someone for months, learn the sound of their footsteps, know which cupboard they opened in the morning and which objects they left beside their beds, and then never discover what happened to them.

The girl wondered whether they remembered her.

Perhaps they remembered the headphones. Or the terrible coffee. Or the fifth bed, which hadn’t officially belonged to anyone.

Perhaps they didn’t remember her at all.

She couldn’t blame them. She had forgotten many people herself.

Still, she found it strange that five people could spend so much time in one room and leave with almost nothing belonging to one another.

Except, perhaps, the smell of melted cheese.

· · ·

Many years later, after she had travelled through several countries and acquired Mr. Rabbit, she would sometimes remember the boarding house.

She would remember the two hills, the endless climbing, the clouds above the third girl’s head, and the first girl’s extraordinary interest in other people’s souls.

She would remember the little kitchen, the pasta, the coffee that apparently tasted terrible, and the Nibbling that had eaten the door handle.

She would also remember the fifth bed.

Not the first one.

The second one.

The storage bed.

Sometimes she wondered whether the boarding house had ever corrected its records.

Perhaps it still contained four inhabitants and five beds.

Perhaps another girl had arrived and bought a place that did not officially exist.

Perhaps the Registrar was still investigating.

She liked to imagine that it was.

Once, Mr. Rabbit asked her why she always placed her suitcase beside the bed when they arrived somewhere new.

– Because that’s where it belongs.

– But you have a cupboard.

– I know.

– Then why don’t you use it?

The girl considered the question.

She had been carrying her belongings from place to place for so long that unpacking had begun to seem like a rather ambitious commitment.

– I might need to leave, she said.

– Where?

– I don’t know.

– When?

– I don’t know that either.

Mr. Rabbit looked at her for some time.

Then he climbed into the suitcase and settled between two folded sweaters.

– Well, he said, at least I’ll be ready.

The girl closed the suitcase halfway, leaving enough space for his ears.

She went to sleep.

During the night, the suitcase moved slightly closer to the door.

Neither of them mentioned it in the morning.
