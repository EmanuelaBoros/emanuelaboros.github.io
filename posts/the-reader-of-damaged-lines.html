The Reader of Damaged Lines

A story, a little imagined · nine short chapters

Once there was a woman whose work was to read lines that had been damaged, and who gradually discovered that the damaged lines were mostly hers.

I. The scanner room

present dayAt the end of a long corridor, in a library that smelled of warm dust and cold coffee, there was a room with one flatbed scanner and one window that faced a wall. The woman worked there. Boxes came to her from places that had flooded, burned, or simply been forgotten, and her job was to teach a machine to read what was left.

The machine was fast and confident and often wrong. It would look at a page and say, in its flat voice, this is what it says, and it would say it with the same calm whether the page was clear or half eaten by damp. That was the part she could not get used to. A person who is unsure hesitates. The machine never hesitated. It just handed you a sentence.

One morning a box arrived with no label. Inside were pages in a handwriting she knew at once, because it leaned the way her own leaned. She turned the first one over. At the bottom, in pencil, someone had written the date of every line.

She sat down and did what she always did. She put the first page on the glass and pressed the button.

II. Rn or m

The machine read the first line and did not blink.

In the town of Vi11e there lived a girl who could not tell the difference between a rn and an m.

The woman laughed, because the machine had made exactly the mistake the sentence was about. It had read the word Ville as Vi11e, two ones standing where two small letters should be. Any person would have seen it in a second. But a search for Ville would now find nothing. The town had vanished from the archive, quietly, without a single alarm going off.

She looked at the page itself. The two letters were not clean. The pen had pressed too hard, stopped, pressed again. Someone had hesitated there.

She was about to correct it, the way she corrected a hundred things a day, and then she stopped. The mistake was not only a mistake. It was the exact place on the page where a hand had shaken. If she cleaned it, the shake would be gone, and with it the only honest trace of how that sentence had been written.

So she did something she had never done. She kept the wrong version, and next to it she wrote the right one, and she drew a thin line between them so that nobody would ever have to wonder which was which.

Noise is not always a bug, she thought. Sometimes it is the witness.

III. The rabbit for fifteen lei

20 July 2010The next pages were from a summer. A girl was walking home with a song stuck in her ears and a smear of foam on one shoe, tired in the sudden way you are tired when you have not slept enough and do not know why you woke.

Near her building, in the window of a second-hand shop, there was a rabbit. It was a stuffed one, a little grey, with the kind of face that has been loved by someone else first. The label said nineteen and a half lei. For a moment it seemed to her that the rabbit glanced at a very small watch, said something about being late, and hurried off through the door. She went in after it, the way girls in old books always do.

“If you want it, I’ll give it to you for fifteen.”

“Really?”

“Really.”

She had the building association’s money in her bag, the money for the stairwell light and the lift, three hundred and fifty lei of other people’s patience. She looked at the rabbit, and at the money, and she thought, calmly, that she would pay most of it today and the rest another day. Two hundred and fifty of three hundred and fifty. Which was very good, she decided. Which was, for a person who had just bought a toy with the neighbours’ lift money, almost responsible.

“I’m buying myself a toy,” she told the man, and he laughed so loudly that the whole shop turned.

She carried the rabbit home under her arm. At the door her tomcat was waiting, who had missed her more than he would ever admit, and who sniffed the rabbit for a long time and then sat down beside it like a guard.

The woman in the scanner room put the page down. In the margin, in pencil, someone had written: I think this is when I stopped being afraid of being ridiculous.

She checked the date. She checked it twice. It was a very long time ago.

IV. The lantern

25 July 2008Two years earlier, on a page torn from a notebook, there was a very short story, and she read it aloud, since nobody was there to hear.

“Mister Rabbit, it is so dark! Haven’t you got a lantern?”

“Of course I have! But to find it, I first have to light the lantern.”

She read it again. Then she looked at the window that faced the wall, and at the scanner with its little green light, and at the box she had spent a career not opening.

She understood, slowly, the way you understand a joke that has been waiting for you, that this was the whole of her problem and probably of everyone’s. You cannot find the light without the light. You cannot read the damaged line without already knowing what it says. The machine did it by guessing. She did it by guessing too, only with a kinder face.

The rabbit in the story, she noticed, did not seem very worried. He just stood in the dark with his lantern, which was somewhere, and waited for the next thing to happen.

V. The tram

11 October 2008At seven in the morning, in a tram so full that nobody could tell their own elbow from a stranger’s, a girl was pushed into a seat between two other girls and found, in front of her knees, a person.

She never saw the person’s face. It was hidden under a hood, turned away, pressed between a bag and a window. She saw jeans, a jacket, one hand holding a strap too tightly. That was all. And for a few stops, in all that noise, in all that shoving, there was nothing else in the world but a small exchange of warmth between two people who did not speak.

The girl did not fall in love. She was careful to write that down. She only noticed that the whole thing was easier because she could not see the face. In her dreams, people rarely said what she wanted in the voice she wanted. Here, nobody said anything at all, and it was almost perfect.

The woman in the scanner room felt the page go warm in her hand. She wondered what the machine would make of it. It would probably find the words, and miss the tram.

She thought: A reader that can recognise every letter and still cannot recognise a morning like this is not a reader. She wrote it on a small yellow note and stuck it on the scanner, where she would have to look at it every day.

VI. The jump

2008, againFurther down the box there was a page of arithmetic about courage. The girl had worked out a theory. She called it, with great seriousness, the theory of jumps.

It went like this. If you have to cross a pit, you should jump once and properly. Many small jumps are worse, because somewhere in the middle of the second or the third you will fall in. A big jump is frightening only before you take it.

She had underlined the next sentence twice: Change the things before you change yourself.

The woman read it, and then she read a page from a few years later, in which the same girl had taken the big jump after all. She had crossed a border with a suitcase and a laptop and a head full of other people’s voices. She had cut her hair. She had sat in a small room in a city where nobody knew her, with her face to the door, and she had said, aloud, to nobody: This is going to be all right. It was not a promise. It was only the first line of a draft.

Still, the woman thought, she jumped.

VII. Letters

14 April 2024The pages from this year were typed and they were shorter, as though the girl had grown tired of ink.

Some people stayed. Strangely, the ones I met recently. That does not mean they will stay.

Many people left, most of them. Is this what I was dreaming of when I was young? To meet people who don’t give a damn? I guess so. That was it.

I loved letters. You wrote one or two a year, and then you met the person once a year at a holiday, and it was enough. Now everyone expects a message once a week, once a day, once a month. I do not have the strength to update everyone about the nonsense I do, or to ask about theirs. I am already bothered by everything I can watch on every screen I own.

I became a little more drastic over the years. Not more direct. I was always direct, a quality people seem not to appreciate.

So, not to lose my chain of thought. Oh. Too late. I lost it.

The woman smiled, in spite of everything, at that last line. It was the most honest thing the machine would never be able to read.

That evening she did something she had not done in a long time. She took a sheet of paper and a pen, and she wrote a letter. Not a message. A letter, with a beginning and an end, to one person, who would receive it in about a week and be very surprised.

VIII. The new clothes

6 March 2008At the very back of the box, folded very small, was a page copied out in the girl’s hand, the old story of the emperor and the weavers. She remembered it, as everyone does. Two swindlers promise a cloth so fine that only the clever can see it. The emperor sees nothing, and is afraid of being called a fool, so he praises it. His ministers see nothing, and are afraid too, so they praise it. The whole town praises it. And the emperor walks out in the procession in his underwear, and nobody says a word, until a child does.

She had underlined something near the end. “There is no cloth,” a child said. And beside it, in pencil: Fine. But what if the child is also afraid?

The woman set the page down next to the scanner. She thought of a model she had read about that week, a large one, that could write a perfectly smooth line over a gap and be completely wrong. It sounded so sure. Everyone around it said how beautiful the cloth was. Some of them, she was sure, saw nothing at all.

A good reader, she decided, is the child. A good reader says: there is a gap here. Here is what I guess. Here is where I got it from. And a good reader keeps the line between the guess and the page, so that the next person can check.

IX. No shame

20 November 2025The last page in the box was not old. The ink had barely dried. It was only a few lines, as though the girl, who was no longer a girl, had run out of room, or patience, or both.

Yeah, looking back, I realised I am not ashamed. I am not ashamed of my mistakes. I am not ashamed of making mistakes.

But all the others are. And this is a problem.

The woman read it three times. Then she put it on the glass, pressed the button, and waited for the machine to tell her what it said.

The machine said it very clearly, and for once it was almost right. It had changed one word. Where the girl had written mistakes, it had written mistales, as if it had mistaken a small k for a smaller l and had grown a fairy tale in the middle of a sentence.

She kept it.

She kept the wrong word and the right word, with a thin line between them, and a note in the margin of the file, in plain letters, for whoever came after her. It said:

This is a damaged line. The damage is part of it. Nothing here needs to be hidden, and nothing here needs to be ashamed.

Then she turned off the scanner, and the little green light went out, and the room, for a moment, was dark. She smiled. She knew exactly where the lantern was.

She had only to light it first.
