The House of Unfinished Mornings

A story, a little imagined · two visits to a house

Somewhere between the City of Hours and the Country of Mistales stands a house where people are sent when the ordinary world becomes too difficult to live in.

Nobody knows who built it.

The house has many rooms but only one day. The day repeats itself, and the people inside wait for the Keepers to bring them the key to the following morning.

The first time the girl arrived, she was told that she would get better.

This was reassuring, although nobody could explain what better meant.

The house stood at the edge of a forest, behind an iron gate that opened only inwards. It had a great many windows, all facing different directions, and a large clock above the entrance.

The clock had no hands.

The girl asked why.

– To prevent people from becoming impatient, said the Keeper.

She thought this was reasonable, although she had never known a clock to cure impatience by refusing to tell the time.

Mr. Rabbit was not allowed in. Animals, said the Keeper, belonged outside. The Mistale, which was larger than the door, sat down on the gravel and said it would wait. Mr. Rabbit said he would wait too, but not for long, and not quietly.

· · ·

Inside, the Keeper gave her a bed, a cupboard and a silver needle.

The needle was placed directly through a little drawing on her arm. The girl watched with interest.

Nobody asked what the drawing meant.

This disappointed her slightly. She had spent some time choosing it.

Her room had two other beds.

In the bed on the right lived a woman who owned very little but required an extraordinary number of cigarettes. In the bed on the left lived a girl who spoke quietly and seemed to have misplaced a considerable part of herself.

The girl introduced herself.

The woman on the right asked for a cigarette.

The girl on the left asked whether they could be friends.

The girl from Sadbury found the second request more difficult.

· · ·

On the first evening she discovered that time behaved differently inside the house. It did not pass. Instead, it arrived in the shape of an enormous grey creature with twelve legs and a clock where its mouth should have been.

It climbed onto her shoulders.

– You will never leave, it whispered.

She tried to shake it off.

– That is a rather dramatic conclusion, she said. I have only just arrived.

The creature did not answer. It was already asleep.

She wondered whether she was expected to carry it all night.

She was.

The creature was called Tomorrow. Everyone in the house had one, in a different size and temperament. The cigarette woman’s was thin and talkative. The priest’s, it was said, had left years ago and nobody had dared to ask why.

· · ·

In the morning the girl found that the house possessed a magical window.

Through it she could see people getting married, baptising children, eating birthday cakes, and smiling in front of things that did not seem particularly amusing. The window showed only these. Nobody in it was ever waiting for a key.

She watched for a long time.

Then she sent a picture of the silver needle in her arm through the window. She thought it might be an interesting contribution.

Nobody appeared particularly impressed.

The woman on the right asked for another cigarette.

The girl began to suspect that cigarettes were the real currency of the house. She wondered whether she could save enough of them to buy an extra morning.

She could not. She tried.

· · ·

The girl on the left followed her everywhere.

When the girl from Sadbury brushed her teeth, the girl on the left brushed hers. When the girl from Sadbury went outside, the girl on the left went outside. She walked a little behind, copying the angle of her shoulders, the way she paused before doors.

– Why do you do that? asked the girl from Sadbury, eventually.

– I lost my shadow, said the girl on the left, very quietly. I thought if I copied yours for long enough, I might remember how it goes.

The girl from Sadbury looked at the floor. Hers lay there politely, with its arms folded.

– It isn’t very good, she said. I think it is mostly slouching.

– That’s all right, said the girl on the left. Mine was mostly slouching too.

She asked the girl from Sadbury to keep what she had said a secret. Then she asked again, in case it had not worked. By the end of the week she was asking every few hours, and the girl from Sadbury, who had already agreed forty times, understood that it was not a request but a way of holding on to something.

· · ·

At night the Nightkeepers came.

They were very small and very quiet, and they carried little silver boxes. Each box held a fragment of sleep, measured exactly, not a crumb more. The girl learned to recognise the sound of their footsteps.

At first she had been afraid of the boxes. Later she began waiting for them. They were the only reliable way of making a day end.

Sometimes the Nightkeepers were late. On those evenings Tomorrow grew heavier.

– Perhaps they have forgotten us, it whispered.

– Perhaps, said the girl.

She had begun to understand that arguing with Tomorrow was a waste of time.

Which was unfortunate, because time was the one thing she possessed in abundance.

· · ·

In the evenings there was a song on a small, crackling radio in the corridor, and a girl from a room down the hall who sat in front of it and cried.

She dreamed of an enormous wedding. She had been dreaming of it for eleven years, and in her version the church had four hundred guests, a cake the size of a bus, and a groom who had not yet been decided. During the day she made invitations for people who did not exist yet. They were very beautiful. She never finished one.

The girl from Sadbury sat down beside her and listened to the song.

Soon both of them were crying.

They did not cry about the same thing. The girl from Sadbury could not have said what hers was about. But the song was long and it was kind, and for four minutes it asked nothing from anybody.

· · ·

On the bench in the garden sat a rook.

He was an old black bird in a priest’s collar, and he had been given the responsibility of hearing confessions. He would much rather have told jokes. They were not good jokes. A few of them were frankly inappropriate, and the nurses coughed pointedly whenever he began one.

– Come and sit, he said to the girl. There is room. There is always room for people who are not in a hurry.

– I am in a hurry, she said.

– Then you have come to the wrong house.

She sat. He told her a joke about a bishop and a hat. She did not laugh, but she noticed that she wanted to, which was something.

When she left the garden he blessed her, a little crookedly. Then he gave her three more blessings, on the house, for the road. She put them in her pocket. They were warm, and heavier than they looked.

The house had its cruel Keepers and its kind ones, and sometimes they were the same Keeper on different days. There were people in it who were wise and people who were silly and a man who could only sit and look at the wall. Some of the people there were kinder to the girl than people she had known outside the gate, and she did not know what to do with that, so she wrote it down.

· · ·

Then one morning the Keeper came with a key, and said the girl might go.

She was surprised. The clock still had no hands. Tomorrow was asleep on her shoulders, and it woke up, looked around, and slid off.

– Goodbye, said the girl on the left, who had found a small, uncertain shadow somewhere between the second and third floors. It was not an excellent shadow, but it was hers.

– Goodbye, said the girl from Sadbury. Keep it close.

Outside the gate, the Mistale stood up. Mr. Rabbit had been sitting on its head for six weeks, composing complaints.

– Well? he said. Better?

– Different, said the girl.

– That’s not an answer.

– No, she agreed.

She walked away through the forest and did not look back. She was quite sure she would never see the house again.

The house, however, had an unfortunate habit of reappearing.

· · ·

Some years later she found it at the end of a road she had not meant to take. It stood at the edge of the same forest, behind the same iron gate. It was a little smaller. Or she was a little older. Or both of them had been worn down by the same weather.

The clock above the door still had no hands.

The Keeper was a different Keeper. The bed was a different bed. The cupboard was the same cupboard, and when she opened it, there was a sock in it that she was almost certain she had left behind.

The woman on the right was gone, although a cigarette lay on the pillow as if someone had meant to come back for it. The bed on the left was empty and made. The girl from Sadbury looked at it for a while, and hoped the shadow had held.

When she turned around, Tomorrow was sitting on the windowsill.

It was thinner than she remembered. It had one leg fewer. It looked at her with its clock-face mouth, and the clock, for the first time, said something.

– Ah, said Tomorrow. You are back.

– Only for a little, said the girl.

– That is what they all say.

It climbed onto her shoulders, as it had before. It was lighter this time, or she was stronger. She was not sure which. She made a note to find out.

– You will never leave, it whispered.

– I did, once, said the girl.

– Did you?

She thought about this for the rest of the week, which, in that house, took considerably longer than seven days.

She did leave. Again, and again after that. The Nightkeepers’ boxes grew fewer, and then she stopped needing them quite so much. She forgot the sound of their footsteps, and one day she realised she had.

Years went by in the ordinary world, where days have a way of ending by themselves.

· · ·

And then, one afternoon, a very long time afterwards, she was standing on a platform in a station she did not know, waiting for a train, and for a letter that would not arrive.

Something climbed onto her shoulders.

It was quite small now. It had four legs, and a clock that ticked a little, on the wrong side.

– You will never leave, it whispered.

The girl recognised the voice.

– Oh, she said. It’s you again.

She picked up her bag. She did not shake it off. She did not argue. She had learned that arguing with Tomorrow was a waste of time, and that carrying it was a smaller waste.

The train came in. She stepped onto it.

This time, Tomorrow had to hold on.
