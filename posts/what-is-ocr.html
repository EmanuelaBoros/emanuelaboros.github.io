What is OCR, and why does it misread old newspapers?

In plain words · November 2026 · about 2 minutes

OCRscanningold print

OCR is the step that turns a scanned page into text a computer can search. On old print it makes mistakes, and those mistakes travel.

When a library “digitises” a newspaper, it first takes a photograph of every page. A photograph is not text: you cannot search it, copy it or feed it to a language model. OCR (optical character recognition) is the software that looks at the picture and writes down the letters it thinks it sees.

Why old pages are hard

Modern OCR is very good on clean, modern print. Historical pages are different: the paper is yellowed or stained, the ink bled through from the other side, the typeface is unusual, and columns are packed tightly together. The software confuses shapes that look alike, such as l and 1, or the old long ſ and f.

Why it matters

A small error at the start becomes a bigger one later. If a name is misspelled in the text, a search will not find it, and a system that looks for people and places will not recognise it. Much of my work starts from this point: what can we still do with text that is a little wrong, and how do we avoid trusting it too much?
