The Man with the Little Black Beast

A story, a little imagined · the city between the two hills

Long before the girl met Mr. Rabbit, and before she discovered that mistakes could become living creatures, she befriended a man who kept a small black beast in his house.

This happened in the city between the two hills, where she was studying two things that had very little to do with each other.

She had met the man outside the School of Pictures, on a morning when everybody was pretending not to be nervous about an examination. He was tall and broad-shouldered, with long hair, heavy boots, and a collection of black clothes that appeared to have been manufactured in anticipation of somebody’s funeral.

The girl liked him immediately.

She liked most people immediately, actually, although she would later develop the rather unfortunate habit of claiming otherwise.

At the time, she believed people were generally as they appeared. If somebody smiled, she assumed they were pleased. If somebody offered friendship, she assumed they wanted to be friends. And if somebody told her something, she tended to believe it, unless it contradicted something she knew for certain.

She did not know very many things for certain.

This made life rather interesting.

The man belonged to a group of creatures who dressed in black, listened to music that sounded like furniture being dragged across a stone floor, and spoke enthusiastically about bands whose names were almost impossible to pronounce.

The girl liked their music.

Not all of it, of course. Some sounded like a large animal trying unsuccessfully to escape from a metal cupboard.

But she liked discovering things.

The man was happy to show her more.

★ ★ ★

One evening, he invited her to his house.

It stood on a narrow street beneath the Hill of Pictures, between a bakery that sold bread shaped like human organs and a watchmaker who refused to repair watches because he believed time should not be encouraged.

The girl had never visited the watchmaker, but she admired his principles.

The man’s apartment was full of peculiar objects.

There were shelves of records, posters of creatures with enormous teeth, books about things that had happened hundreds of years earlier, and an impressive collection of black garments hanging from the backs of chairs.

The girl looked around with interest.

She liked other people’s houses.

They were full of evidence.

A person’s possessions revealed things they might never say directly, although interpreting the evidence was not always straightforward. A book could mean somebody had read it, intended to read it, or wanted visitors to believe they had read it.

She had learned this after buying several books herself.

The man showed her his collection of music.

He placed a record on an enormous mechanical player, and a sound emerged that reminded the girl of thunder rolling down a staircase.

She sat on the floor.

– Do you like it? he asked.

– Yes.

He smiled.

The girl was pleased.

She liked making people happy, particularly when it required very little effort.

Then something moved beneath a chair.

She turned.

Two small black eyes were watching her.

– What is that?

– My beast.

The creature emerged slowly.

It was round and covered in dark fur, with enormous ears, tiny paws, and a tail resembling a feather duster that had survived a particularly violent storm.

The girl had never seen anything quite like it.

– Is it a rat?

– No.

– A squirrel?

– No.

– A very small rabbit?

The man looked offended.

– It’s a Chinch.

– A what?

– A Chinch.

The creature sat on its hind legs and stared at her.

She stared back.

It seemed an agreeable arrangement.

★ ★ ★

The Chinch lived in a large cage filled with wooden houses, little tunnels, and things intended to be chewed.

It was allowed out occasionally, although the man warned her that it might bite.

– Does it bite often?

– Sometimes.

– Why?

– Because it’s an animal.

The girl considered this a rather inadequate explanation.

She had met many animals. Some bit, others didn’t. A few seemed to be perfectly capable of biting but preferred to spend their time sleeping.

The Chinch approached her.

She held out her hand.

It sniffed her fingers and immediately began chewing the sleeve of her coat.

– Stop that.

The Chinch continued.

– That’s my coat.

The creature appeared unimpressed by the distinction between ownership and availability.

She looked at the man.

– Does it eat everything?

– Almost.

– Then it’s fortunate you still have furniture.

The man laughed.

The girl laughed too.

She liked the Chinch very much.

★ ★ ★

They spent the evening listening to music.

The man knew an extraordinary number of bands. Some had released only one record, others had never performed in public, and several appeared to have existed exclusively in the imaginations of the people who admired them.

The girl was impressed.

She had always admired people who knew things she didn’t.

Sometimes she admired them before discovering what those things were.

The man explained the difference between several kinds of music.

She listened carefully.

When she didn’t understand, she asked.

He seemed to enjoy explaining.

This was pleasant.

The girl often found herself in conversations where people expected her to know things already. She preferred conversations in which ignorance could be corrected without becoming a personal defect.

At some point, the Chinch climbed onto her knee.

It examined her trousers, considered eating them, and eventually decided to sit there instead.

The girl felt honoured.

She had always found the affection of animals particularly reassuring. Animals did not generally ask whether she was ambitious enough, social enough, attractive enough, or sufficiently grateful for the opportunities she had been given.

Some expected food, of course.

But their expectations were usually explicit.

The Chinch remained on her knee for nearly an hour.

She became afraid to move.

★ ★ ★

As the evening continued, the man told her stories.

He spoke about the city, the school, people he knew, and things he hoped to do.

The girl listened.

Sometimes she imagined the places he described. Other times she imagined entirely different places, which was a habit she had possessed since childhood.

She had always lived partly in her head.

As a little girl, she could spend an afternoon inventing the history of a stone, or imagine that a stranger on the street had once been a king. She could construct entire conversations with people who had only said hello.

These imaginary conversations were generally much more successful than real ones.

In them, she always remembered her arguments.

And other people always had time to listen.

The man interrupted her thoughts.

– Are you listening?

– Yes.

– What did I say?

She repeated his last sentence.

He laughed.

– You’re strange.

– Probably.

She did not mind.

She had never considered strange a particularly serious accusation.

The Chinch, meanwhile, had fallen asleep inside her coat sleeve.

★ ★ ★

The girl returned to the man’s house several times.

Sometimes they listened to music. Sometimes they talked. Sometimes they sat quietly, which she preferred.

She began to think of him as a friend.

This happened rather naturally.

She did not keep a register of friendships or require people to sign agreements. If she enjoyed somebody’s company and they seemed to enjoy hers, she assumed friendship had occurred.

She was not entirely wrong.

But she had a tendency to imagine the best version of people before she knew them particularly well.

The man, for example, was funny, generous with his music, and patient when explaining things.

She therefore imagined he was also kind in situations she had not witnessed, fair toward people she had not met, and thoughtful about matters they had never discussed.

There was no particular evidence for any of this.

She simply liked him.

And liking somebody made imagining the rest rather easy.

★ ★ ★

One evening, the Chinch was unusually restless.

It ran around the room, climbed the furniture, and repeatedly attempted to eat a corner of the carpet.

The man placed it back in its cage.

The creature protested by throwing a small wooden object across the floor.

– What’s wrong with it? asked the girl.

– Nothing.

– It seems unhappy.

– It’s always like that.

The girl watched the Chinch.

She wondered whether the creature was unhappy or simply had a different understanding of happiness.

She herself had often been told that she looked unhappy when she was merely thinking.

The man returned to his chair.

They began discussing another student from the School of Pictures.

The girl knew her slightly.

She was quiet, dressed rather plainly, and rarely joined the others when they went out.

The man said something about her.

It was a small remark, delivered casually, as though it required no particular thought.

In this telling, the remark concerned whether somebody who was so peculiar deserved to be included in the group.

The girl looked at him.

She had not expected this.

– Why wouldn’t she?

The man shrugged.

– Come on. You know what I mean.

She didn’t.

Or perhaps she did, but disliked what she understood.

He laughed, apparently expecting her to laugh too.

The girl remained silent.

For a moment, the room seemed strangely different.

The records were still there. The black clothes hung from the same chairs. The music continued playing, and the Chinch was making a tremendous noise inside its cage.

Nothing had changed.

Except that the man had said something she wished he hadn’t.

★ ★ ★

The girl thought about the remark on her way home.

Perhaps he had been joking.

She liked jokes.

She was not always good at recognising them immediately, but once she understood, she generally appreciated the effort.

Perhaps he hadn’t meant it.

Perhaps she had misunderstood.

There were many possibilities.

She considered them all.

Then she remembered his expression.

She remembered how easily the words had come out, and how confidently he had expected her agreement.

That troubled her more than the words themselves.

She wondered whether he spoke similarly about her when she wasn’t there.

This was an unpleasant thought.

She had no evidence that he did.

But she had no evidence that he didn’t.

For the first time, she began to understand why people sometimes preferred not to know things.

★ ★ ★

Several days passed.

The man sent her a message, asking whether she wanted to visit again.

She looked at it.

She thought about the music, the conversations, and the little black creature that had slept inside her sleeve.

She wanted to see the Chinch.

She was less certain about the man.

This seemed unfair to the Chinch, who had done nothing wrong.

She considered going.

Then she imagined sitting in the room again, listening to him explain things while remembering the remark.

She suspected she would spend the entire evening examining everything he said for evidence of something she had previously overlooked.

That did not sound particularly enjoyable.

So she declined.

He asked why.

She said she was busy.

This was not entirely true.

She had discovered that honesty was sometimes a great deal more complicated than she had imagined.

She did not particularly enjoy the discovery.

★ ★ ★

Time passed.

She continued attending the two schools, running up and down the hills, making friends and occasionally losing them.

She met other people who introduced her to things she had never heard of.

Some were wonderful.

Others were not.

A few were wonderful in certain circumstances and thoroughly unpleasant in others.

This last category troubled her.

She had grown up believing that people possessed something resembling a fixed nature.

The princess, for example, had always insisted that there were good people and bad people, and that recognising the difference was essential.

Unfortunately, the princess’s classification frequently changed depending on whether somebody agreed with her.

The girl had never found the system reliable.

Now she began to suspect that people were rather like houses.

You could spend an entire evening in a beautiful room without knowing what was stored in the basement.

Sometimes there was nothing particularly interesting.

Sometimes there were mice.

And sometimes the basement was simply another room, perfectly ordinary, that you happened not to like.

She wondered whether the man had been unkind or whether she had expected too much.

She never reached a satisfactory conclusion.

★ ★ ★

Years later, she came across his picture.

He looked almost exactly the same.

The same long hair, the same black clothes, the same expression.

She found this remarkable.

In the years since she had last seen him, she had lived in several cities, changed her mind about countless things, and acquired an impressive collection of experiences she would rather not repeat.

And there he was, looking as though he had merely stepped into another room.

She wondered whether he still listened to the same music.

Whether he had become kinder.

Whether he remembered her.

And, rather unexpectedly, whether the Chinch was still alive.

She could no longer remember the creature’s name.

This made her sadder than she had anticipated.

She could remember the texture of its fur, the shape of its ears, and the way it had examined her coat as though it were edible.

But not its name.

She tried to remember.

Nothing came.

She thought about contacting the man to ask.

Then decided that this would be a rather peculiar reason to reopen a correspondence after so many years.

Hello. I haven’t spoken to you in a decade, but what was your rodent called?

She laughed.

Then she closed the picture.

★ ★ ★

Much later, when Mr. Rabbit had become her travelling companion, the girl told him about the little black beast.

Mr. Rabbit listened carefully.

He was particularly interested in the part where the creature had tried to eat her coat.

– Did it succeed?

– No.

– That’s unfortunate.

– Why?

– Perhaps it was hungry.

– It had food.

– Perhaps it preferred the coat.

The girl considered this.

Mr. Rabbit frequently defended animals with unreasonable appetites.

She suspected he was afraid that somebody might eventually question his own usefulness.

– Do you think I was wrong to stop seeing the man? she asked.

Mr. Rabbit examined his paws.

– Did you like him?

– Yes.

– Did he hurt you?

– Not exactly.

– Did you want to see him again?

– Not particularly.

– Then what’s the problem?

The girl thought about this.

She had been trying to decide whether the man was good or bad, whether the remark had revealed something essential about him, and whether her disappointment had been justified.

Mr. Rabbit, however, appeared completely uninterested in the man’s moral character.

He wanted to know why she was still discussing somebody she hadn’t seen for years.

This was irritating.

It was also a reasonable question.

★ ★ ★

That night, the girl dreamed of the little black beast.

It was sitting inside the man’s apartment, surrounded by records and old clothes.

The man was nowhere to be seen.

The Chinch had grown enormous.

Its ears touched the ceiling, its tail stretched through the hallway, and its body was covered with thick black fur.

It was eating the books.

– Stop that, said the girl.

The creature looked at her.

– Why?

– Because they’re not yours.

– Whose are they?

– The man’s.

– Where is he?

– I don’t know.

– Then perhaps they’re mine now.

The girl considered arguing.

Instead, she sat on the floor.

The creature approached and placed its enormous head on her knee.

Its fur was exactly as she remembered.

She stroked it.

For some time, neither spoke.

Then she asked, Do you remember me?

The beast considered this.

– Should I?

– No.

– Then perhaps I do.

The girl smiled.

She had forgotten how agreeable animals could be.

★ ★ ★

In the morning, Mr. Rabbit was sitting on the windowsill.

– Did you dream? he asked.

– Yes.

– About me?

– No.

He looked offended.

The girl got out of bed and made coffee.

She thought about the man once more.

She remembered the music and the evenings she had enjoyed. She remembered the remark that had disappointed her.

Neither memory had disappeared.

She supposed they could both be true.

Outside, a small bird was attempting to pull a worm from the ground. The worm was resisting with admirable determination.

The girl watched them.

Eventually, the bird gave up.

The worm disappeared.

Mr. Rabbit looked at the window.

– Who won?

– The worm, I think.

– How do you know?

– It got away.

– Perhaps the bird wasn’t hungry.

– Perhaps.

– Perhaps it didn’t like worms.

– Then why was it pulling one out of the ground?

Mr. Rabbit thought for a while.

– Maybe it wanted to meet it.

The girl laughed.

It was a ridiculous explanation.

But she liked it.

She finished her coffee, picked up Mr. Rabbit, and went outside.

She had somewhere to go, although she couldn’t remember exactly where.

This was not unusual.

And somewhere, in a house she would never visit again, there might once have lived a small black beast whose name she had forgotten.

She hoped it had been a pleasant life.

She hoped it had eaten many expensive coats.
