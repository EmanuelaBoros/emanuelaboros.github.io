The Examination of Moving Things

A story, a little imagined · two schools, two hills

There were two schools on opposite hills in a city not far from Sadbury.

One taught people how to prove that things existed. The other taught them how to see things that might not.

The girl applied to both.

She did not see a contradiction in this, although almost everybody else did.

At the School of Numbers, admission depended on the correct arrangement of certain symbols. The girl liked symbols. They behaved predictably, and when they didn’t, there was usually a reason.

The School of Pictures was more complicated.

Nobody could agree on what a picture was supposed to contain, but everybody seemed remarkably confident in their ability to recognise a good one.

The girl found this suspicious.

Nevertheless, she wanted to attend.

She had always liked drawing, although she preferred the act of drawing to explaining what the drawing meant. Once, when asked why she had painted a tree blue, she had answered that she had run out of green.

This had apparently been an inadequate artistic statement.

She had considered inventing a more sophisticated explanation, but the tree was already dry.

· · ·

On the morning of the entrance examination, the girl put on her favourite trousers.

They were pink, with enormous pockets, and had originally been designed for soldiers.

She liked them because they were comfortable and because she could carry an unreasonable number of things without needing a bag.

The princess had once suggested that they were inappropriate.

The girl had not understood what kind of trousers were appropriate for an examination in which one was expected to demonstrate creativity.

Perhaps black trousers.

People seemed to consider black a particularly serious colour.

She wore the pink ones anyway.

· · ·

The School of Pictures occupied a large building whose entrance was guarded by two stone lions. One lion had its eyes closed. The other had no eyes at all.

Above the entrance was written:

ONLY THOSE WHO CAN SEE MAY ENTER.

The girl examined the lions.

She wondered whether either of them had been admitted.

Inside, a long corridor was filled with applicants. They carried portfolios, enormous tubes of paper, brushes, boxes and mysterious instruments that looked as though they belonged in a dentist’s office.

Some spoke confidently about perspective.

Others discussed light.

One young man had painted half his face blue.

The girl wondered whether this was necessary.

She had brought nothing except her identification papers, cigarettes and the contents of her enormous pockets.

She found a place near the window.

Beside her stood two young men.

One was tall, with long hair and a black coat. He looked as though he had recently attended a funeral, although the girl suspected this was simply his preferred appearance.

The other was examining a crack in the wall.

– Do you think they’ll ask us to draw? asked the first.

– Probably, said the girl.

– I heard we have to photograph something.

– That would be nice.

– Do you have a camera?

– No.

He looked at her.

– Neither do I.

They considered this.

The boy examining the wall announced that the crack looked like a small dragon.

The girl went over to inspect it.

It did.

Or rather, it looked like a crack that might become a dragon if one were sufficiently determined.

She liked that.

· · ·

When her turn came, a woman with six hands opened the examination-room door. She wore an enormous black hat and spectacles with lenses of different colours.

– Next.

The girl entered.

The room contained two tables.

On the first lay several cameras, some so large that they required their own chairs.

On the second stood a collection of objects: wooden animals, little statues, glass bottles, an artificial tree, three apples and something resembling a human ear.

The woman pointed to the cameras.

– Choose.

The girl looked at them.

She had never used most of these instruments. One had twelve buttons. Another contained a small door through which she could see nothing. The largest produced a faint humming sound, as though it were thinking about something unpleasant.

She chose a modest black camera with a comfortable strap.

– Why that one? asked the woman.

– It looks easiest.

The woman made a mark on a sheet of paper.

The girl immediately regretted her answer.

Perhaps she should have said that she was interested in its technical properties.

She was, in fact, interested in its technical properties. She simply didn’t know what they were.

– Your task, said the woman, is to photograph movement.

The girl looked at the table of objects.

Nothing moved.

– May I move something?

– Of course.

– May I move anything?

– Within reason.

The girl disliked this qualification.

Reason, she had discovered, was a remarkably flexible thing.

She picked up a wooden horse and placed it on the edge of the table. The horse had three legs and a slightly offended expression.

She moved it with one hand while trying to hold the camera with the other.

The result was unsatisfactory.

The photograph showed half a horse and an enormous pink sleeve.

She tried again.

This time the horse fell off the table.

The woman with six hands looked up.

– Careful.

– Sorry.

The girl retrieved the horse.

It had lost an ear.

She placed the ear beside it, thinking that perhaps the examiner wouldn’t notice.

– Don’t repair it, said a little voice.

The girl turned around.

There was nobody there.

– Down here.

Beneath the table sat a small creature with six legs and no visible head. Its enormous stomach occupied most of its body, and in the middle of the stomach was a mouth.

It was chewing one of the table legs.

– What are you doing? whispered the girl.

– Eating.

– You’re not supposed to be here.

– Neither are you, probably.

The girl considered this.

– Are you a Nibbling?

– Obviously.

– Could you stop eating the table?

– Why?

– Because I need it.

– So do I.

The creature continued chewing.

The table began leaning to one side.

The girl looked at the examiner.

The woman was occupied with three other applicants, holding a camera with one hand, adjusting a light with another, and writing something with a third. The remaining hands appeared to be waiting for instructions.

The girl turned back to the Nibbling.

– You’re going to make everything fall.

– Then take your picture.

She looked at the table.

The wooden horse was trembling. The glass bottles were slowly sliding toward the edge. One of the apples had begun rolling.

Suddenly, everything fell.

The girl pressed the button.

There was a tremendous crash.

The examiner turned around.

The Nibbling disappeared beneath a cupboard.

For a moment, nobody moved.

The girl stood holding the camera. At her feet lay three broken bottles, a wooden horse, two apples and the artificial tree.

The third apple had escaped beneath the door.

The woman approached.

– What happened?

The girl considered explaining the Nibbling. But the creature had already gone, and she suspected that blaming a small animal with a mouth in its stomach would not improve her chances of admission.

– The table fell.

– Why?

– One of its legs was shorter.

The woman examined the table.

Indeed, one leg was now considerably shorter than the others.

She looked at the girl.

– Did you take the photograph?

– Yes.

– May I see it?

The girl handed over the camera.

The woman studied the image.

Then she called another examiner. He was a thin man wearing a scarf despite the warm weather.

They looked at the photograph together.

The girl watched their faces.

Neither smiled. Neither frowned.

The man pointed at something. The woman nodded.

Then they whispered.

This was unpleasant. She had always preferred people who expressed their opinions at a volume she could hear.

Finally, the woman handed back the camera.

– Thank you.

– Is that all?

– Yes.

– Should I put the objects back?

– No.

The girl looked at the broken bottles.

– Sorry about the table.

– It happens.

The examiner opened the door.

The girl left.

· · ·

In the corridor, the two young men were waiting.

– How did it go? asked the one in black.

– I broke a table.

– On purpose?

– No.

– That’s unfortunate.

– Probably.

The boy examining the crack looked interested.

– Did you photograph it?

– Yes.

– Then perhaps that’s what they wanted.

The girl shook her head.

She was fairly certain that destroying furniture was not a standard requirement for admission to the School of Pictures.

Then again, she had never attended an art school.

Perhaps it was.

She went outside and lit a cigarette.

The two boys joined her.

For some time, they stood together without speaking.

The girl liked this. She was beginning to discover that the best conversations sometimes involved very little conversation.

The boy in black asked whether she wanted to see his photographs.

She did.

They were rather good.

He had photographed a small wooden man jumping from one chair to another. The movement was blurred, and the little figure appeared to have several arms.

The girl was impressed.

– How did you do that?

– I moved him.

– Of course.

She felt foolish.

She showed him her photograph.

It contained a falling apple, a crooked table, and something that might have been the tail of a small creature disappearing beneath a cupboard.

The boy examined it for a long time.

– What’s that? he asked, pointing to the tail.

– I don’t know.

– It looks like an animal.

– Perhaps.

– It’s funny.

The girl smiled.

This was the first positive assessment of her work she had received that day.

· · ·

A few weeks later, she returned to the city to see the examination results.

The names of the successful applicants had been written on a large sheet of paper and attached to the wall.

People crowded around it.

Some were delighted. Others looked disappointed. One young woman was crying while a friend explained that the examination had been unfair.

The girl waited until the crowd became smaller.

Then she approached the wall.

She began reading.

There were many names.

She read them all.

Then she read them again.

Her own was near the bottom.

She looked at it carefully.

It was definitely hers.

She had been admitted.

For a moment, she felt an extraordinary warmth somewhere beneath her ribs.

She had expected failure so completely that success seemed to require an explanation.

Perhaps the examiners had made a mistake.

Perhaps there was another girl with the same name.

Perhaps they had liked the photograph.

She looked again.

The name remained.

This was reassuring.

She took a small piece of paper from her pocket and wrote it down, although she knew perfectly well what her name was.

Then she folded the paper and put it away.

On her way home, she encountered the boy in black.

He had been admitted too.

– So, he said, the table worked.

– Apparently.

– Are you going to study here?

– Yes.

– Only here?

– No. I’m also going to the School of Numbers.

The boy stared at her.

– Why?

– I like numbers.

– And pictures?

– Yes.

He thought about this.

– That sounds exhausting.

– Probably.

They walked toward the station together.

At a crossing, the girl stopped to examine a peculiar machine attached to a lamppost. It had several wheels, a glass tube, and a little brass plate announcing that it measured the amount of daylight remaining.

She looked at it for some time.

– Does it work? asked the boy.

– I don’t know.

– Can you find out?

– Probably.

– How?

She began explaining.

It would require observing the readings, comparing them with the actual light, determining whether the machine measured brightness or time, and possibly opening it.

The boy listened.

Then he looked at the sky.

– Or we could just wait until it gets dark.

The girl considered this.

He was right.

She laughed.

It was an unexpectedly good laugh, the kind that began somewhere in the chest and escaped before she could decide whether it was appropriate.

The boy laughed too.

Neither knew exactly what was funny.

They continued walking.

· · ·

Many years later, after the girl had travelled through several countries and acquired a rather opinionated toy rabbit, she would sometimes remember the examination.

She never remembered what the examiners had said about the photograph. She couldn’t even remember whether the image had been particularly good.

She remembered the pink trousers.

The broken table.

The little Nibbling chewing its leg.

And the strange warmth of finding her name on the wall.

Mr. Rabbit was fascinated by the story.

– You were admitted because an animal ate the furniture?

– Possibly.

– That’s ridiculous.

– Yes.

– Did you ever thank it?

– No.

– You should have.

The girl thought about this.

She had never considered thanking the Nibbling. After all, it had simply been eating.

She wondered whether it was still alive somewhere, chewing its way through the School of Pictures.

Perhaps the building had collapsed.

Perhaps the examiners had finally learned to photograph movement.

Perhaps nobody had noticed the creature at all.

One afternoon, while walking through a distant city, she saw a small round animal beneath a bench.

It was chewing the pavement.

She stopped.

– Excuse me.

The creature looked up, or at least turned its stomach in her direction.

– Have we met?

– Probably not.

– Did you once eat a table?

– Many.

– At a school?

– Several.

The girl considered asking more questions, but realised that the answers were unlikely to help.

– Well, she said, thank you anyway.

The Nibbling looked offended.

– For what?

– For something you might have done.

– That’s a stupid reason to thank somebody.

– Yes, I suppose it is.

The creature returned to its meal.

Mr. Rabbit looked at the girl.

– Did you get what you wanted?

– Not really.

– Then why are you smiling?

She did not know.

They walked away.

Behind them, the pavement gave a small cracking sound.

Neither turned around.
