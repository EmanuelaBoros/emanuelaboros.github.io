The Mistake Collector

A story, a little imagined · 2008 – 2025

2008Long before she learned what freedom could cost, the girl from Sadbury found a small creature living inside a book.

It was an unpleasant-looking thing, with three legs, one ear, one eye larger than the other, and a tail that seemed to have been attached at the wrong end. It was sitting in the margin of page forty, crying.

– What’s wrong? asked the girl.

– Everything, it said.

She examined it carefully, the way she examined everything.

– Well, you seem to be missing a leg.

– I know.

– And one of your eyes is larger than the other.

– I know.

– And your tail—

– Would you please stop?

The girl was surprised. She had only been trying to help.

– Sorry.

The creature wiped its nose on the page.

– Everyone does that, it said. They look at me and tell me what is wrong. Nobody ever asks where I came from.

– Where did you come from?

The creature thought for a while.

– I don’t remember. I think I used to be a sentence.

– A sentence?

– A perfectly ordinary one. But somebody decided I was not good enough, crossed me out, and wrote a nicer one on top.

– What happened to the nicer sentence?

– Oh, it is doing very well. Everybody likes it.

The girl looked at the three legs, the one ear, the tail.

– Perhaps, she said, you were just a sentence somebody didn’t like.

The creature stopped crying. Nobody had ever suggested this before.

It was called a Mistale, it explained, when she finally asked. Mistales were born whenever somebody corrected something that had not necessarily been wrong. A child told that her feelings were inappropriate made one. A memory rewritten to be more pleasant made another. An embarrassing evening, pretended away, left a little Mistale behind to carry off whatever remained.

– And where do they go?

– To the Country of Mistales, said the creature. It has been removed from all the maps. It is very crowded.

She tried, that first week, to improve it. She straightened the tail. The ear promptly vanished. She gave it back its fourth leg, drawn in pencil, and the eye on the left went missing. After that she stopped trying, and the creature, which had never been followed by anybody, began following her.

It kept things in its pockets, although it had no pockets. When she looked closely, she found small crumpled fragments of everything she had once said and wished she hadn’t. She decided not to look closely very often.

Years went by the way years do when you are small and busy: without anyone noticing, and all at once.

11 October 2008One morning, at seven, the girl found herself in a carriage so full that nobody could tell their own elbow from a stranger’s. She was pushed into a seat between two other girls, and in front of her knees stood a passenger.

She never saw the passenger’s face. There was not one. Where it should have been there was a sort of soft grey, like a window in fog. The other passengers had none either, and nobody seemed to mind. They were, in fact, surprisingly pleasant company. Nobody asked where she was going. Nobody wanted an explanation. They sat together and swayed together, touching a little whenever the carriage turned, and one by one they stepped out at their own stops.

The girl became attached to the passenger in front of her knees. She had no right to be. They never spoke. There was a hood, a jacket, a hand holding a strap too tightly, and a small, unspoken exchange of warmth that required nothing from anyone.

She wrote this down that night, carefully, so that she would not forget. She noticed, with some alarm, that she had forgotten how she felt.

– You see, said the Mistale from the corner of her desk, this is what it is like.

– What is it like?

– Keeping a thing without being able to keep it.

The next morning the carriage was gone. The stop was there, and the pavement, and a man selling plums, but no carriage and no faces. The girl stood for a long time looking at the place where it had been.

Then she walked on, with her Mistale behind her, a little taller than before.

20 July 2010In a market that sold only things that had already belonged to somebody else, the girl found a grey rabbit.

It was a stuffed one, a little worn, with the kind of face that has been loved by someone else first. It sat in a basket between a clock without hands and a coat with a great many buttons. The label said nineteen and a half coins.

– If you want me, said Mr. Rabbit, I’ll go for fifteen.

– You can speak?

– I have been waiting for you, said the rabbit. I cannot remember why. My name is Mr. Rabbit, by the way.

The girl had money in her bag that was not hers. It had been entrusted to her for something considerably more important: a lamp for the stairs, a lift that would never work, the patience of a great many neighbours. She considered this carefully. She decided that she would pay most of it today and the rest another day, which was, for a person about to buy a rabbit with other people’s lamp money, almost responsible.

– I’m buying myself a toy, she told the stallholder, and he laughed so loudly that the whole market turned.

As they were leaving the market, the girl noticed a small, round creature beneath a bench. It had six legs, no visible head, and was eating the cobblestones.

– Why are you eating the road? she asked.

– Because it’s there.

– But people need it.

– Then they should have eaten it first.

The girl considered this.

– What are you?

– A Nibbling.

– Is that your name?

– No. That’s what I am.

– And what do Nibblings do?

The creature looked at the half-eaten road.

The girl felt rather stupid.

Mr. Rabbit was offended. The Mistale was fascinated.

– Perhaps it’s a mistake, suggested the Mistale.

– Not everything strange is a mistake, said the girl.

The Mistale looked rather pleased.

The Nibbling continued chewing until a small hole appeared in the street. Then it rolled onto its back, revealing a mouth in the middle of its stomach, and fell asleep.

– That’s a very strange animal, said Mr. Rabbit.

– I think I like it, said the girl.

– Why?

– It doesn’t seem particularly concerned about being liked.

Mr. Rabbit thought this a poor quality in a companion.

The girl picked him up and tucked him under her arm. He found the position comfortable and continued travelling that way for years, offering advice, nearly all of it impractical, and always at the most inconvenient moment.

The Mistale did not like Mr. Rabbit at all. It said Mr. Rabbit was smug. Mr. Rabbit said the Mistale was damp. They sat on opposite shoulders and argued about this for years.

25 July 2008On the night the road ran out and the forest began, it was Mr. Rabbit who said, Mister, it is so dark! Haven’t you got a lantern?

– Of course I have, said the Mistale, who had spent a great deal of time thinking about this. But to find it, I first have to light the lantern.

They all three sat in the dark for a while, which was fair, since none of them could find the light without already having it.

2008Then the road came to a ravine.

It was enormous. The far side was so distant that it looked painted on. A bridge of flat stones ran across it, though not exactly: the stones appeared only when somebody decided to cross.

Other travellers were attempting it. They went carefully, one small step after another, and each stone sank beneath them with a polite little sigh.

The girl watched for some time. She had a theory, which she had worked out on the back of a page, and which she called the theory of jumps. If you have to cross a pit, you should jump once and properly. Many small jumps are worse, because somewhere in the middle of the second or the third you will fall in. A big jump is frightening only before you take it.

– Change the things before you change yourself, she told Mr. Rabbit.

– That sounds like something you read, said Mr. Rabbit.

– I wrote it.

– Then it sounds like something you wrote.

She was terrified. She took off her shoes, tucked Mr. Rabbit under one arm and the Mistale under the other, and she jumped.

She landed in a city where nobody knew her name. It had a river, and a great many cranes, and a small room at the top of a tall house. She cut her hair. She sat in the room with her face to the door and said aloud, to nobody in particular, This is going to be all right.

It was not a promise. It was only the first line of a draft.

Years passed. She did not count them, which is how she knew they were passing. Her coat grew longer, then shorter, then longer again. She learned several languages and forgot two of them. Somewhere along the way she began to be called Doctor, which amused Mr. Rabbit enormously, and then she began to be called other things that amused nobody. The Mistale, she noticed one winter, had grown quite large. It no longer fitted on the desk. It slept across the foot of the bed with all four of its legs, which had arrived without anyone drawing them.

She was no longer particularly young. The creatures still called her the girl.

14 April 2024In the middle of that long stretch there was a post office.

It stood on a hill at the end of a street that did not appear on any other street’s map. Inside, the letters took years to arrive. Some reached people who had since died. Some were delivered to strangers who happened to share a name. Others returned to their writers with replies that had never been written, in handwriting that looked a great deal like their own.

The postmaster was an enormous owl in a very small hat.

– Correspondence, he told the girl, has become a terrible burden.

– Has it?

– People used to write because they had something to say. Now they write to demonstrate that they have not disappeared.

The girl thought of the messages waiting on the stairs behind her, the daily, the weekly, the monthly, all asking after her nonsense and hoping to hear about hers. She thought of the long letters she used to write, one or two a year, and how that had been enough.

– I prefer the old system, she said.

– So do I, said the owl. Unfortunately, I am not permitted to prefer anything. It is one of the conditions of the post.

He stamped a sheet of paper, looked at it, and slid it across the counter.

It was a letter, addressed in her own hand to somebody she had not seen in years.

– Is it too late? she asked.

– Possibly, said the owl, but the postage has been paid.

She took it with her. She wrote one back that evening, with a beginning and an end, and posted it herself. Somewhere a person would receive it in about a week and be very surprised.

It was on the road from the post office, a good while later, that the girl came to the country at the end of the maps.

She had not meant to go there. It was simply that the road went down, and then down a little more, and then there was a gate with nobody at it and a sign that said, in very neat letters, WELCOME. The word had been written, rubbed out, and written again, many times. Under the paper-thin surface of the sign, you could see all the earlier versions.

The Country of Mistales was crowded, as promised.

Everything that had ever been corrected seemed to have ended up here. Unfinished letters sat on the park benches. Forgotten names wandered the streets, looking for the people who had once owned them. Friendships that had not quite ended, and had not quite continued, queued outside a grey building with no door. And everywhere, in alleys and fountains and the folds of the hills, were the Mistales themselves: three-legged, squinting, patched, enormous, wrong. Some were selfish. Some were rude. One had been selling the same umbrella to the same visitor for eleven years. Several did not like her at all.

– Ours is bigger, said one small Mistale, pointing at the girl’s.

– Ours has been in a book, said Mr. Rabbit, whose snobbery was fully intact.

– Does it do tricks?

– It cries, said Mr. Rabbit.

– That is a trick, said the small Mistale, impressed.

The girl walked through all of it with her mouth slightly open. She had not known so many people had been crossed out.

At the top of the country, in a white house with perfectly straight windows, lived the Great Corrector.

It was a magnificent monster. It could make anything appear perfect. It straightened crooked houses, mended broken statues, took the sting out of old arguments, and even improved conversations that had already taken place. Everybody admired it, and, to be fair, it helped people. A man who had been unable to look at his father’s grave could now look at it, and bring flowers. A mother who had said an unkind thing at the wrong moment could now remember saying a kind one. Nobody who left the white house seemed any worse for it.

– Visit me, said the Corrector, in a voice like a very clean window.

She did. She was shown, with great tenderness, a small gallery of her own history, arranged so that nothing in it hurt. It was beautiful. There was the room in the tall house, but warm. There was the tram, but with faces. There was a jump she had not been afraid to take. Not a single letter was unanswered.

– You may have it, said the Corrector. It will take no time at all.

– What happens to the old one?

– Nothing happens to it. It simply ceases to be the one you remember.

The girl looked at her gallery. She was a little embarrassed to find that she wanted it.

Then she noticed something about the people in the corridors of the white house. They all had beautiful memories. And none of them could say exactly what had happened.

She thought of her Mistale, large and patient, sitting outside on the steps with Mr. Rabbit. She thought of what it carried in its pockets.

– No, thank you, said the girl.

– Everybody says that the first time, said the Corrector gently, and it was not unkind.

She went down the hill, and the white house shone behind her, perfectly straight.

She returned to that country many times. She did not always want to. It was only that her road kept leading there, the way a road leads past a house where you used to live. She bought umbrellas. She was rude to a Mistale who did not deserve it, and did not apologise, and did not much mind. She made friends with two of them and a stray name, and lost a day arguing about something that turned out not to matter. She was impatient, and wrong, and sometimes simply wanted a thing that made no sense. In this respect she was no better than the creatures, which was precisely why they let her in.

20 November 2025The last time she went, it was winter.

By then the Mistale was very old, and so large that it could no longer fit through the gate. It sat outside, steaming gently in the cold. It contained, by now, everything she had never managed to correct about herself: the sentence she should not have said, the evening she should have left earlier, the friend she should have written to, the years that had been harder than they needed to be. She could not have told you which of these were mistakes and which were simply things that had happened. It had never mattered to the Mistale.

The Great Corrector came out to meet her on the steps.

– I have a final offer, it said. All of it. Every one. Gone in a moment.

The girl looked at the old creature, then at Mr. Rabbit, now bald in places, then at her own hands.

She was not ashamed. She was not ashamed of her mistakes, and she was not ashamed of making them. It was the others who were ashamed, on her behalf, and that was a different problem and not hers.

– Not because every mistake was good, she said. Some of them were stupid. But they happened. If you take them away it will not make me someone else. It will only leave holes in the story.

– Holes can be mended.

– I know. That is what frightens me.

The Corrector bowed its enormous head. It had never lost an argument and it did not lose this one. It simply stepped aside, as a sensible person does, to let a very stubborn traveller go past.

On the way back she passed the post office, closed for the season. She passed the ravine. Its stones were back, small and flat, waiting for somebody to decide. She passed the market, and the stall where a rabbit had once cost fifteen coins, and the stop where a carriage had once stood. She had lived a number of years in all these places without noticing. She noticed now.

It was evening when she reached her own door. She was tired. The Mistale, which had followed her all the way, sat down heavily on the step and looked at her with its two unmatched eyes.

– What do you think? she asked Mr. Rabbit.

– I think, said Mr. Rabbit, you ought to call it something other than the Mistale.

– What would you call it?

Mr. Rabbit considered this.

– Once, you wrote a word wrong, it said. Years ago, in a notebook. You meant mistakes and you wrote mistales, as if one small letter had grown a fairy tale in the middle of a sentence. You crossed it out, and then you didn’t cross it out, and you kept it.

– I kept it, said the girl.

– That was the beginning, said Mr. Rabbit. That is where he came from.

She looked at the creature on the step. Three legs, then four. One ear, then two. An enormous face that had been, a long time ago, a perfectly ordinary sentence.

She had not corrected it. She had only learned how to read it.

She sat down beside it. For a while neither of them spoke, and the dark came down over the street.

– It is very dark, said the girl. Haven’t we got a lantern?

– Of course we have, said Mr. Rabbit.

– But to find it, said the Mistale, in the voice of someone who has finally been asked, you first have to light it.

So she did.
