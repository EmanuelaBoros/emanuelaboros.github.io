The Kingdom of Borrowed Wings

A story, a little imagined · mid-January 2023 – mid-January 2026

mid-January 2023Beyond the well, where the forest became too thick for the sun to enter, there was a kingdom in which every creature was free.

This was written on a large stone at the entrance.

The girl stopped to read it.

Underneath, in much smaller letters, someone had added:

Freedom is subject to the conditions of the kingdom.

She thought this was reasonable. Most things were.

She had been travelling for a long time, carrying the half wedding dress her grandmother had never finished, Mr. Rabbit, a toy she had once bought for fifteen lei, and a collection of things she could not throw away. Mr. Rabbit was particularly useless, but she had never required usefulness from rabbits.

At the gates stood two enormous birds with human hands and no eyes. They asked what she was carrying.

– Nothing important.

– Then you may enter, said the first bird.

– What if I had said something important?

– Then we would have had to examine it.

She was glad she had answered correctly.

Inside, the kingdom was beautiful. Trees grew upside down, their roots reaching into the clouds. White deer walked on their hind legs, carrying little lanterns between their antlers. In the evenings, enormous moths descended from the mountains and covered the houses like blankets.

There was a lake in the middle of the kingdom, so perfectly still that the girl suspected it was not water at all.

She liked the lake.

She liked the mountains too. They reminded her of places she had already been, although she could not remember which ones. This had begun to happen more often. She had lived in so many cities that sometimes she remembered the window of one apartment from the bedroom of another.

· · ·

The kingdom was ruled by the Lady of Feathers.

She was a magnificent creature, half woman and half bird, with enormous silver wings and a crown made of very small mirrors. When she walked, the mirrors reflected the faces of everyone around her, so that whoever looked at the Lady saw a little of themselves.

People said this made her exceptionally understanding.

The Lady welcomed the girl warmly.

– We have heard of you, she said. You have travelled through Sadbury, the City of Hours, and the Tower of Control. You know how to find things that have been lost.

– Not always, said the girl.

– How modest.

– I wasn’t being modest.

– Even better.

The Lady invited her to remain in the kingdom. She would be given a small room in the eastern tower and permission to wander through the gardens.

There was only one condition.

She had to learn to live among the creatures.

The girl agreed.

She had been trying to learn this for most of her life.

· · ·

At first, she was happy.

She wandered through the gardens, followed the strange little beasts that lived in the hedges, and discovered paths that seemed to lead somewhere until one actually took them.

She made friends with a creature called a Nibbling.

Nibblings were round, furry animals with six legs and a mouth in the middle of their stomachs. They ate fallen leaves and occasionally their own feet, although the feet grew back.

The girl found this practical.

One morning she discovered that the Nibbling had eaten the path leading to her room.

– Why did you do that?

– It was in my way.

– But now I can’t get home.

– That is not my concern, said the Nibbling.

She thought this was a rather good answer, although she would not have dared to give it herself.

· · ·

The other creatures were less straightforward.

The deer spoke only in questions. The moths agreed with everything, even when they had not understood what was said. A family of blue monsters who lived beneath the stairs communicated entirely by changing colour.

The girl tried to learn their customs.

When a deer asked whether the weather was pleasant, she answered yes.

The deer looked offended.

Apparently, she was supposed to answer with another question.

When a moth asked whether she liked the kingdom, she said that she liked the lake very much but found the stairs rather inconvenient.

The moth repeated this to another moth, who told a third moth, who informed the Lady that the girl was unhappy.

The girl was surprised.

She had thought she was making conversation.

One afternoon, at a feast, a small creature with three heads asked whether she wanted another piece of cake.

– No, thank you.

All three heads went silent.

The girl wondered whether she had offended them.

She had.

The proper answer was to accept the cake, thank the creature, and place it discreetly beneath the table.

– But why?

– Because refusing cake is unkind.

– And throwing it away?

– That is different.

She tried to remember the rule.

There were many such rules, although none had been written down. The girl, who had always liked lists, considered writing them herself.

But whenever she thought she had understood one, somebody explained that it had never been a rule at all.

· · ·

Several months passed.

One evening, the Lady of Feathers summoned her to the Hall of Echoes.

The hall had no windows and was lined with enormous mirrors. The girl could see herself in every direction, sometimes taller, sometimes shorter, sometimes with her mouth in the wrong place.

The Lady was sitting on a golden chair. The cupboard behind the throne was shut, and she spoke as though the whole hall belonged to her.

– We need to discuss the trouble you have been causing.

The girl looked behind her.

There was nobody else.

– What trouble?

The Lady sighed.

– You see? This is exactly the trouble.

The girl waited.

The Lady explained that several creatures had felt uncomfortable in her presence.

– Which creatures?

– That is not important.

– What did I do?

– It is difficult to explain.

– Then how can I stop doing it?

The Lady looked disappointed.

The girl recognised this expression. The princess in Sadbury had worn it often, usually just before explaining that the girl had failed to understand something perfectly obvious.

– There have been incidents, said the Lady.

The girl asked for an example.

After some hesitation, the Lady mentioned the feast.

Apparently, the three-headed creature had been deeply hurt by her refusal of cake.

– But I thanked him.

– Yes, but you refused.

– I didn’t want cake.

– That is not the point.

The girl wondered what the point was.

She did not ask.

There had been other incidents, the Lady continued. Some creatures thought her too quiet. Others thought her too direct. One had complained that she looked distracted during the annual procession of the Moon Beetles.

The girl remembered the procession.

She had been watching a particularly interesting cloud shaped like a horse.

She had not realised the beetles required continuous attention.

The Lady explained that nobody had mentioned these things before because the creatures feared the girl might be upset.

This puzzled her.

They had apparently spent months being upset by the possibility that she might become upset.

She considered telling the Lady that she was not particularly upset.

But this might have upset her.

· · ·

After that meeting, the girl began to change.

She spoke more when she thought she ought to speak, and less when she thought she ought to be quiet. She smiled at the deer, accepted cakes she did not want, and learned to look attentively at processions even when the clouds were more interesting.

Sometimes she got the rules wrong.

Sometimes the rules changed.

Once, a moth invited her to a gathering in the northern garden. She went, carrying a small lantern.

The Lady later informed her that she should not have gone.

– But I was invited.

– Of course.

– Then why shouldn’t I have gone?

– Because you were not expected.

The girl thought about this for a long time.

It reminded her of the well in the thirsty forest, which had been full of water but would give her none until she was going home.

She had disliked the well then.

Later, she had returned and found it perfectly reasonable.

Perhaps the same would happen with the kingdom.

She waited.

· · ·

The strangest creature in the kingdom lived in a cupboard behind the throne.

It was called the Listener.

Nobody had ever seen its body, but enormous ears emerged from the cupboard at irregular intervals. They were soft, pink, and covered in little golden rings.

The Listener knew everything.

Or at least everyone said so.

The girl was never allowed to speak to it. Whatever she said had to be carried to the cupboard by the Lady, who whispered it through the door, and what came back out was never quite what had gone in.

It repeated her words in a different order.

In its presence, the Lady changed. Her silver wings folded. Her crown of mirrors tilted toward the floor, so that for once it reflected nothing but the carpet. Her voice, which was usually so firm, became very small.

– I would like to go walking in the forest, she said once, to the Lady.

The Lady bowed to the cupboard and whispered. Then the Listener emerged.

– She feels imprisoned, it announced.

– No, said the girl. I only want to go walking.

– She denies feeling imprisoned.

The creatures exchanged concerned looks.

The girl tried again.

– I like it here. I would simply like to explore the forest on my own.

– She feels that the kingdom is preventing her from becoming herself.

This was becoming difficult.

– Could you please repeat what I actually said?

She had said it to the cupboard. The Lady answered first, with her head still bowed.

– One does not speak to the Listener directly.

The Listener withdrew into the cupboard. The door closed. The wings opened again, one feather at a time, and the Lady straightened until she was her own height, and then a little more.

– Don’t make me do that again, she said, very sharply.

Then she looked at the girl sadly.

– You must learn to trust the Listener.

The girl had no objection to trusting a creature with enormous ears. But she was beginning to suspect that the ears were mostly decorative.

· · ·

One day she noticed that all the birds in the kingdom had ribbons tied around their wings.

Some were red, some yellow, and some almost invisible.

The ribbons were very beautiful.

They were also very short.

The birds could fly from one branch to another, but never beyond the gardens.

– Why don’t you fly farther? she asked a white owl.

The owl looked horrified.

– We are free to fly.

– But the ribbons.

– They protect us.

– From what?

– From flying too far.

The girl considered this.

She had always thought that the purpose of wings was to fly too far.

She asked the Lady whether the ribbons might be loosened.

The Lady smiled.

– Of course. We encourage freedom.

– Then may I go beyond the gardens?

The smile disappeared.

– Why would you want to leave?

– I don’t want to leave. I want to see what is beyond them.

– Are you unhappy here?

– No.

– Then why do you need more?

The girl did not know how to explain that wanting more space was not the same as wanting to disappear.

She had spent years trying to find a room of her own. Now she had one, but it seemed that having a room meant she was expected to remain inside it.

She wondered whether the Lady had ever tried to fly with her own wings.

They were enormous, but the girl had never seen her use them.

· · ·

That evening, the girl returned to the eastern tower.

She sat beside the window and began writing a letter.

She explained that she liked the kingdom. She described the strange rules, the misunderstandings, the cakes she had been obliged to accept, and the difficulty of knowing whether an invitation was really an invitation.

She wrote that she had never wanted to hurt the creatures.

She also wrote that she did not wish to spend the rest of her life apologising for things nobody had explained to her.

The letter became very long.

She crossed out several sentences.

She replaced you are wrong with perhaps there has been a misunderstanding.

She replaced I want with it would be preferable if.

This was an expression she had learned many years earlier, on the other side of the hills. She had never particularly liked it.

By midnight, she had written fourteen pages.

Mr. Rabbit sat opposite her on the table.

– What do you think? she asked.

Mr. Rabbit said nothing.

It had cost fifteen lei, and she did not expect conversation at that price.

She read the letter again.

It was reasonable, polite, and full of good intentions.

She imagined the Lady opening it, reading carefully, and finally understanding.

Then she imagined the Lady opening it, reading the first sentence, and deciding that the girl was once again causing trouble.

She considered rewriting it.

Then something rather simple occurred to her.

The Lady had never asked for an explanation.

She had asked the girl to change.

The girl looked at the fourteen pages.

She had spent an entire evening trying to explain herself to someone who had shown very little interest in understanding her.

She folded the letter.

Not because she was afraid to send it.

She simply decided that the Lady did not deserve fourteen pages of her life.

Nor fifteen.

She placed it beneath Mr. Rabbit and went to sleep.

· · ·

The next morning, the Lady called her to the palace.

The Hall of Echoes was unusually quiet.

Behind the throne, the cupboard door stood open by a finger’s width, and a single pink ear was visible in the gap.

The Lady sat beneath her crown of mirrors, which tilted slightly toward the floor. She looked very solemn.

– I understand that you wish to be free, she said.

– Yes.

– And that you want to go beyond the gardens.

– Sometimes.

The Lady closed her eyes.

It seemed that she was making a very difficult decision. The girl noticed that she was not looking at the girl at all, but at the gap in the cupboard door.

Then she rang a small silver bell.

Two enormous birds entered the hall.

The same birds who had welcomed the girl at the gates.

– The kingdom has decided, said the Lady, to grant you your freedom.

The girl did not understand.

The birds approached her.

– You may leave, said the Lady.

– Now?

– Yes.

– And come back?

The Lady looked away.

The girl stood very still.

She had asked for the ribbons to be loosened.

They had removed her wings instead.

For a moment she thought of explaining this distinction.

She was quite good at distinctions.

Then she remembered the letter beneath Mr. Rabbit.

She said nothing.

The birds led her through the palace.

The corridors were long and full of mirrors. In each one she saw a different version of herself: a little girl, an old woman, a wolf, a princess, a creature with too many arms, and, in the last mirror, a girl holding a half wedding dress.

She stopped.

The reflection stopped too.

It was the first thing in the kingdom that had behaved as expected.

Behind her came the sound of wings.

The Lady of Feathers stood at the end of the corridor.

She smiled.

– Well, she said, I hope you’ve got your freedom now.

The girl turned around.

For a moment she saw something strange.

The Lady’s magnificent wings were not wings at all.

They were made of thousands of tiny feathers, each borrowed from another creature. Some were white, some grey, some almost transparent. Each had been tied carefully to the next with a thread so thin that from a distance the whole construction looked natural.

The girl wondered whether the Lady had ever possessed wings of her own.

Then the doors opened.

And she was outside.

· · ·

mid-January 2026It was winter.

The trees stood naked around the lake. The mountain peaks were covered with snow.

She had not realised how much time had passed.

At the gates, the stone still announced that every creature was free.

One of the eyeless birds was scratching at the smaller inscription, trying to remove a word that had become inconvenient.

The girl watched for a moment.

– Does it hurt? she asked.

– What?

– Not having eyes.

– We have never needed them.

– How do you know where to go?

– We are told.

She almost laughed.

Then she continued walking.

She was angry, although the anger came and went in strange waves. Sometimes she felt nothing at all. At other times she remembered the Lady’s smile and wanted to return simply to tell her that freedom was not something one could give to another creature by throwing it out of the kingdom.

But she did not return.

She had no desire to make the Lady understand.

She had spent enough time on that.

· · ·

That night she slept beneath an enormous tree.

In her dream, she was back in Sadbury, sitting on the floor beside the bed.

The princess was standing over her, very beautiful in her pale dress.

– You see, said the princess, you always want too much.

The girl looked at the bed.

The mouse was there, very small, almost hidden among the pillows.

She wanted to ask him something.

But when she opened her mouth, a white feather came out.

Then another.

Soon the whole room was full of feathers.

The princess began gathering them, arranging them carefully into two enormous wings.

– Look, she said. Now I can fly.

The girl woke before seeing whether she could.

· · ·

In the morning, she found Mr. Rabbit sitting beside her.

This was unusual, because she had left it in her bag.

Perhaps it had fallen out.

Perhaps the Nibbling had followed her and rearranged her belongings.

She picked it up.

Beyond the trees was a road she had never taken.

There were no signs, no ribbons, and no instructions.

She walked along it for some time.

Then she stopped and looked back.

In the distance, the kingdom shone beneath the winter sun, its towers rising above the lake. The creatures would be waking now. The deer would begin asking questions. The moths would agree. The Lady would sit beneath her mirrors, and the Listener would repeat whatever needed repeating.

Perhaps another traveller would arrive at the gates.

Perhaps the Lady would offer her a room in the eastern tower.

Perhaps she would be happy there.

The girl hoped so.

She had never wished the kingdom to disappear.

Only to be allowed to move around inside it.

She opened her bag and found the letter, still folded beneath Mr. Rabbit.

She considered leaving it on the road.

Instead, she put it back.

There was no reason to abandon fourteen perfectly good pages simply because the intended reader had become unnecessary.

She would find another use for them.

Perhaps she could make paper birds.

Perhaps a small fire.

Perhaps nothing.

She continued walking.

After some time, Mr. Rabbit spoke.

– Are we free now?

The girl looked at it.

She had not expected this.

– Apparently.

– That’s nice, said Mr. Rabbit.

– Yes.

– Where are we going?

– I don’t know.

Mr. Rabbit thought about this.

– Are we allowed?

The girl looked at the road, which had begun to divide into several smaller roads, some leading uphill, some downhill, and one disappearing directly into a tree.

– I suppose we’ll find out.

Mr. Rabbit seemed satisfied.

She carried it under her arm, the way she had carried it home from the second-hand shop many years before.

And somewhere behind her, in the kingdom of borrowed wings, the Lady of Feathers was preparing for the annual Celebration of Freedom.

All the birds would attend.

Their ribbons had been shortened for the occasion.
